**Theological Themes and Interpretations**

Matthew Henry emphasizes God's sovereignty and justice throughout this chapter. He notes that in the Shibboleth incident, "The Lord delivered them into my hand," highlighting that Jephthah attributed victory to God rather than himself. Henry identifies pride as the theological root of the Ephraimites' conflict: "Pride was at the bottom of the quarrel. Only by that comes contention." He particularly stresses divine retribution, observing how "the righteousness of God" manifested in the punishment exactly matching their sins - those who called others "fugitives" became fugitives themselves.

**Key Practical Applications**

Henry draws several pointed applications about interpersonal relationships. He warns against "abusive tongues" that give "foul language," noting that "grievous words stir up anger" and can lead to devastating consequences. The commentary emphasizes how "relationship, which should be an inducement to love and peace, should be ever an occasion...of strife and discord." He cautions against taking "pleasure in adding affliction to the afflicted" and highlights how "those resentments that have the least reason for them have commonly the most rage in them."

**Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights**

On verse 1, Henry explains that Ephraim's jealousy stemmed from Jacob's ancient blessing favoring them over Manasseh, creating ongoing tribal tensions. Regarding verses 5-6, he provides fascinating linguistic commentary on the Shibboleth test, comparing it to regional dialects: "as in England we know a west-country man...by his pronunciation." He notes that pronunciation habits learned in childhood became so ingrained that Ephraimites "could not do otherwise, no, not to save their lives." For the final verses about the minor judges, Henry observes that their brief reigns fulfilled the principle: "For the transgression of a land, many are the princes thereof."

**Cross-References**

Henry connects this passage to multiple scriptures: James 3:6 regarding the destructive power of the tongue, Psalm 64:8 about words cutting the throat of their speaker, and Proverbs 28:2 explaining the succession of many short-reigned princes. He references the similar conflict between Ephraim and Gideon (Judges 8:1) and draws parallels to Christ's disciples being identified by speech patterns ("Thou art a Galilean, and thy speech betrays thee").
