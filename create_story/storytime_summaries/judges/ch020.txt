## Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on Judges 20

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as a sobering account of civil war within Israel, emphasizing the tragic irony that such devastating conflict arose "soon after the glorious settlement of Israel in the land of promise." He identifies this as belonging to "the book of the wars of the Lord," though it represents one of the darkest episodes in Israel's history.

The central theological tension involves **righteous zeal versus destructive consequences**. While Henry commends "the pious zeal of Israel against the wickedness of the men of Gibeah, which made it on their side a just and holy war," he mourns the "obstinacy of the Benjamites in protecting their criminals" and the near-extinction of an entire tribe. This demonstrates how even just causes can lead to devastating outcomes when handled improperly.

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons about **corporate responsibility and justice**:

1. **Unity in righteous causes**: The assembly came together "by the consent and agreement, as it were, of one common heart, fired with a holy zeal." Their strength lay in being "knit together as one man" when confronting evil.

2. **Proper judicial procedure**: Despite their zeal, the Israelites conducted a "solemn examination of the crime" and ensured multiple witnesses testified, following the law that "none should be put to death, much less so many, upon the testimony of one witness only."

3. **Timely action**: Henry notes that "when there appears in people a pious zeal for any good work it is best to strike while the iron is hot, for such zeal is apt to cool quickly if the prosecution of the work be delayed."

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides detailed analysis of the assembly's formation (vv. 1-2), noting that **400,000 armed men** represented "not hirelings nor pressed men, but the best freeholders." He explains that Mizpeh was chosen as the meeting place due to its proximity to Shiloh, showing consideration for the priests who served at the tabernacle.

Regarding the Levite's testimony (vv. 4-7), Henry observes the man's modesty in not fully repeating the Sodomites' shameful demands, noting that "his modesty would not suffer him to repeat it." The commentary emphasizes that "**All lewdness is folly, but especially lewdness in Israel**"—a memorable phrase highlighting the heightened responsibility of God's covenant people.

### Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects this account to several biblical precedents:

- **Genesis 19**: The sin parallels Sodom, with Henry noting they "declared their sin as Sodom, even the sin of Sodom"
- **Joshua 4:13**: Referenced regarding military organization
- **Judges 3:1**: Connected to the wars of Canaan
- **Judges 18:29**: Links to the recent naming of Dan

The commentary also alludes to broader biblical principles about witness requirements and the necessity of removing evil from among God's people, themes that resonate throughout Deuteronomy and other legal passages.

Henry concludes with the sobering observation that "all this [was] the effect of the indignities done to one poor Levite and his wife; **so little do those that do iniquity consider what will be the end thereof**"—a warning about sin's far-reaching consequences that extends beyond this historical account to universal spiritual truth.
