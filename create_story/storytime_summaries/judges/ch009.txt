# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Judges 9: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Judges 9 as divine judgment through internal strife rather than foreign oppression, demonstrating how apostasy leads to self-destruction. The central theological theme is that "for the transgression of a land, such are the princes thereof" - God allows wicked rulers as punishment for national sin. 

Henry characterizes Abimelech as "a meteor, this ignis fatuus of a prince" who "came in like a fox, ruled like a lion, and died like a dog." This illegitimate ruler represents the consequences when people abandon God's theocracy. Unlike previous judges called by God for deliverance, Abimelech was driven purely by personal ambition, directly contradicting his father Gideon's refusal of hereditary kingship.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications about leadership and ambition:

**On the dangers of ambition:** Abimelech's story demonstrates "what beasts ambition will turn men into" and how it "will break through all the ties of natural affection and natural conscience." The commentary warns against the corrupting nature of unchecked political ambition.

**On choosing leaders:** The people's preference for Abimelech over qualified candidates illustrates how societies often elevate unworthy leaders while rejecting those truly capable of service. Henry notes that "none would have dreamed of making such a one king, if he had not dreamed of it himself."

**On social position:** Henry observes "the peril of honour and high birth," noting that Gideon's sons' noble lineage made them targets. He counsels: "Let none then envy those of high extraction, or complain of their own meanness and obscurity. The lower the safer."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-6 (Abimelech's rise):** Henry emphasizes how Abimelech manipulated family connections and used fear-mongering about multiple rulers to gain support. The funding from Baal-berith's temple showed his unfitness to lead Israel, as he "made himself a pensioner to an idol."

**Verses 7-21 (Jotham's parable):** Henry praises this as ingenious instruction, noting that "the way of instruction by parables is an ancient way, and very useful, especially to give reproofs by." The parable demonstrates that qualified people often refuse power while unworthy candidates eagerly grasp it.

**Jotham's preface (v.7):** Henry highlights the spiritual principle: "Those who expect God to hear their prayers must be willing to hear reason, to hear a faithful reproof."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this narrative to broader biblical themes:

- **2 Kings 10:1,7** - Parallels Ahab's seventy sons also being slain, showing a pattern of dynastic violence
- **Proverbs 28:9** - "If we turn away our ear from hearing the law, our prayer will be an abomination" 
- **Psalm 104:16** - References to trees as "trees of the Lord which he has planted"
- **Judges 8:35** - Links to Israel's ingratitude toward Gideon's house

Henry also draws connections to the broader cycle of Israel's apostasy and God's judgments throughout Judges, showing how this internal conflict represents a new form of divine chastisement for the nation's spiritual rebellion.

The commentary presents this chapter as a cautionary tale about leadership, ambition, and the consequences of abandoning divine guidance in favor of human political schemes.
