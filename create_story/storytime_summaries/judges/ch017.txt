Here is a concise summary of Matthew Henry's commentary on Judges 17:

## **Main Theological Themes and Interpretations**

Henry emphasizes that this narrative demonstrates the spiritual chaos that occurred "when there was no king in Israel." He views this chapter as illustrating the progressive decay from idolatry to complete moral collapse. The commentary traces how "the beginning of idolatry, as of other sins, is like the letting forth of water: break the dam, and you bring a deluge." Henry sees this as the first recorded instance of Israelite apostasy after Joshua's death, marking how worship of God through images (violating the second commandment) inevitably opens doors to worshipping false gods entirely.

## **Key Practical Applications**

Henry draws several pointed applications: First, he warns against the "love of money" which "destroys the duty and comfort of every relation," using Micah's theft from his mother as an example. Second, he emphasizes the importance of godly government, stating "blessed be God for the government we are under!" and noting that proper "administration of those two great ordinances, magistracy and ministry" supports religion. Third, he warns that "when good ministers are neglected and at a loss for a livelihood," it signals religion's decay.

## **Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights**

On verses 1-6, Henry details the family dysfunction caused by greed - the son's theft, mother's curses, and their misguided "reconciliation" through creating idols. He notes how the mother's covetousness prevailed over her superstition when she used only 200 of 1100 silver pieces for the images. Regarding verse 6's key phrase, Henry observes: "Every man did that which was right in his own eyes, and then they soon did that which was evil in the sight of the Lord."

## **Cross-References and Biblical Connections**

Henry connects this account to 1 Peter 1:18 regarding traditions received from parents, Jeremiah 44:17 on ancestral worship patterns, and 1 Timothy 2:1-2 concerning prayers for governing authorities. He draws parallels between Micah's teraphim and the biblical urim and thummim, and notes how Micah attempted to "mimic and rival both God's oracles and his ordinances." The commentary also references the broader context of Judges 18-21, showing how this initial corruption spread throughout Israel.
