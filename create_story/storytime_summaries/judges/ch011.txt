# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Judges 11: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Jephthah as an example of God's sovereign choice in using unlikely instruments for His purposes. The central theme is **divine providence working through human rejection and humiliation**. Henry observes that "God often humbles those whom he designs to exalt, and makes that stone the head of the corner which the builders refused." He draws parallels between Jephthah and other biblical figures like Joseph, Moses, and David who were "thrust out by men, before they were called of God to their great offices."

Henry interprets Jephthah's illegitimate birth and exile as God's way of **mortifying Israel's pride**. Since Israel had "made themselves children of whoredoms" through idolatry, God deliberately chooses "a bastard and an exile" to deliver them. This reflects the broader theological principle that God uses the despised to shame the mighty.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from the narrative:

**On social treatment of the disadvantaged:** He warns against despising those who are "mean" or taking advantage of others, "because we know not how soon our distresses may be such as that we may be highly concerned to make [them] our friend."

**On bearing mistreatment:** For those who are "slighted or ill-treated," Henry encourages bearing it "with meekness and cheerfulness" and trusting God to vindicate them, noting that "Virtue once in an age will work her own advancement."

**On Christian salvation:** Henry applies Jephthah's conditional leadership to Christ's lordship, asking: "If he save you, will you be willing that he shall rule you? for on no other terms will he save you."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3:** Henry emphasizes that illegitimate birth should not be held against someone striving for personal merit. He notes Jephthah's noble response to exile—his "great soul would not suffer him either to dig or beg"—and suggests his wilderness experience prepared him for leadership.

**Verses 4-11:** Henry highlights the irony of the Gileadites' desperation, comparing it to Israel's relationship with God: "They had thrust God out by their idolatries, yet in their distress begged his help." He praises Jephthah's wisdom in securing clear terms before accepting leadership.

**Verse 11:** Henry commends Jephthah's immediate turn to prayer, showing he "leaned not to his own understanding or courage, but depended on God."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects Jephthah to **Hebrews 11:32**, noting he's "numbered among the worthies of the Old Testament." He references **Deuteronomy 23:2-3** regarding exclusion of illegitimate children from the congregation. He parallels Jephthah's rejection and restoration with **Joseph's treatment of his brothers**. Henry also cites **Psalm 82:3-4** regarding magistrates' duty to "defend the poor and fatherless," and draws connections to **chapters 10 and 12** for contextual understanding of Israel's broader situation during this period.

Henry's commentary reveals Jephthah as a complex figure whose personal struggles mirror Israel's spiritual condition, while demonstrating God's grace in using flawed instruments to accomplish His purposes.
