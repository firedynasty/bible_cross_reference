# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Judges 16: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Samson as "a little sun" whose tragic downfall illustrates the destructive power of unchecked lust. The central theological theme is how sin progressively weakens and ultimately destroys God's servants. Henry emphasizes that **"whoredom is one of those things that take away the heart,"** showing how sexual immorality clouds judgment and spiritual discernment.

Despite Samson's moral failures, Henry maintains a typological interpretation, seeing Samson as **"a type of Christ, conquering by death."** Even in his final destruction, Samson prefigures Christ's victory over death and the grave, with Henry noting that Christ "not only rolled away the stone from the door of the sepulchre...but carried away the gates of the grave, bar and all."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws sharp warnings about spiritual vulnerability. When Samson escapes Gaza at midnight, Henry applies this to all who indulge fleshly lusts: **"O that all those who indulge their sensual appetites...would see themselves thus surrounded, waylaid, and marked for ruin, by their spiritual enemies! The faster they sleep, and the more secure they are, the greater is their danger."**

He emphasizes the progressive nature of temptation, noting that **"his sin began in his eye, with which he should have made a covenant."** The commentary warns against the corrupting power of money, referencing how Delilah was bribed with silver, connecting this to Judas's betrayal of Christ.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3 (Gaza incident):** Henry suggests Samson's midnight awakening came through divine intervention - **"perhaps roused by a dream...by his guardian angel, or rather by the checks of his own conscience."** The carrying away of Gaza's gates symbolizes Christ's victory over death's prison.

**Verses 4-17 (Delilah's deception):** Henry notes the irony that **"Samson, that has more than the strength of a man, in this comes short of the wisdom of a child."** He emphasizes how Delilah's repeated betrayals should have warned Samson, yet he remained "perfectly besotted."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects Samson's story to Solomon's warnings in **Proverbs 6:26 and 7:26** about adulteresses who "hunt for the precious life" and cast down "many strong men." He references **Lamentations 4:7-8** regarding the defiled Nazarite, and **Job 33:15** about divine warnings in dreams.

The typological connection to Christ draws from resurrection imagery, with Henry exclaiming **"O death! where is thy sting? Where are thy gates?"** echoing 1 Corinthians 15:55, showing how Samson's final victory prefigures the believer's triumph over death through Christ.

Henry's commentary ultimately presents this chapter as both a cautionary tale about the devastating effects of moral compromise and a prophetic picture of divine victory through apparent defeat.
