## Matthew Henry Commentary on Judges 19 - Summary

### Historical Context and Main Themes

Matthew Henry frames Judges 19 within the dark period "when there was no king in Israel," identifying it as occurring shortly after Joshua's death when Phinehas was high priest. He presents this chapter as the beginning of a "most tragical story" that demonstrates how moral decay spreads when godly leadership is absent. Henry notes the ironic pattern that "there is no complaint of a public disordered state but there is a Levite at one end of it, either as an agent or as a patient" - here the Levite serves as the victim rather than the perpetrator of evil.

### Key Theological Insights

**On Marriage and Reconciliation (verses 1-3):** Henry commends the Levite's pursuit of his unfaithful concubine, drawing a parallel to God's restoration of backslidden Israel. He notes that "it was a virtue in him to forgive the offence, and, though the party wronged, to make the first motion to her to be friends again." The Levite "spoke friendly to her, or comfortably," which Henry connects to Hosea 2:14 where God promises to "speak comfortably" to adulterous Israel.

**On Parental Responsibility (verse 2):** Henry criticizes the father's enabling of his daughter's adultery: "Perhaps she would not have violated her duty to her husband if she had not known too well where she should be kindly received. Children's ruin is often owing very much to parents' indulgence."

### Practical Applications

**On Hospitality and Travel:** Henry uses the lack of public lodging to appreciate modern conveniences, noting that "there is no country in the world wherein one may stay at home with more satisfaction, or go abroad with more comfort, than in our own nation." He condemns the coldness of Gibeah's inhabitants toward the traveling Levite.

**On Separation from Worldliness:** Henry praises the Levite's refusal to lodge with the Jebusites, stating he "would by no means quarter, no, not one night, in a city of strangers," demonstrating proper separation from ungodly influences. Ironically, Henry observes that "debauched and profligate Israelites are worse and much more dangerous than Canaanites themselves."

**On Unintended Consequences:** Regarding the father-in-law's excessive hospitality that delayed their departure, Henry warns that "our friends' designed kindnesses often prove, in the event, real injuries; what is meant for our welfare becomes a trap."

### Notable Cross-References

Henry connects the narrative to several biblical passages: Jeremiah 3:1 regarding remarriage after divorce, Hosea 2:14 on God's restoration of Israel, Luke 24:28-29 showing Christ's accommodation to his disciples' requests, and Isaiah 56:12 warning against false security about the future.

This chapter serves as Henry's introduction to demonstrating how moral corruption spreads in the absence of godly authority, setting the stage for the horrific events that follow.
