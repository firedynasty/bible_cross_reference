# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Judges 4: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Judges 4 as following the familiar biblical cycle of apostasy, oppression, repentance, and deliverance. He emphasizes **the destructive nature of spiritual backsliding**, noting "the strange strength of corruption, which hurries men into sin notwithstanding the most frequent experience of its fatal consequences." 

A central theme is **God's sovereignty in both judgment and deliverance**. When Israel forsook God, "he forsook them; and then they became an easy prey to every spoiler." Conversely, God orchestrates their salvation through unlikely instruments - a woman prophet and a reluctant military leader.

Henry highlights **divine providence working through complementary gifts**: "He could do nothing without her head, nor she without his hands; but both together made a complete deliverer." This demonstrates that "the greatest and best are not self-sufficient, but need one another."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed lessons:

**The dangers of prosperity**: "The common ill effects of a long peace" made Israel "secure and wanton." He warns that "the prosperity of fools destroys them" and that spiritual vigilance weakens during comfortable times.

**The importance of godly leadership**: Israel "did evil, because Ehud was dead." Henry observes how people often fear human leaders "more than God," showing "the great loss which a people sustains by the death of good governors."

**Seeking God in extremity**: Though people "slight God in their prosperity," they inevitably "find themselves under a necessity of seeking him when they are in trouble."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides rich detail on **Deborah's character and calling** (vv. 4-5). Her name means "bee," and "she answered her name by her industry, sagacity, and great usefulness to the public." He notes she judged "not as a princess, by civil authority conferred upon her, but as a prophetess, and as God's mouth to them."

Regarding **Barak's conditional response** (v. 8), Henry defends it as arising "from a conviction of the necessity of God's presence and continual direction," rather than weak faith.

The **prophecy about Sisera's defeat by a woman** (v. 9) demonstrates God's sovereignty over seemingly contingent events.

## Cross-References to Scripture

Henry weaves numerous scriptural connections throughout:
- Links Israel's rebellion to Deuteronomy's warnings about prosperity (Jeshurun waxing fat)
- References Jeremiah 11:15 about God's alienation from unfaithful people
- Connects to Genesis 9:25 regarding the Canaanites' destined servitude
- Cites Micah 4:11-12 and Isaiah 8:9 about God gathering enemies for their destruction
- References Revelation 19:17-18 on divine judgment

Henry's commentary masterfully demonstrates how this historical narrative reveals timeless spiritual principles about human nature, divine justice, and God's faithfulness to his covenant people despite their repeated failures.
