# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Judges 10 - Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes God's **cyclical pattern of judgment and mercy** throughout Israel's history. The commentary highlights how Israel's repeated apostasy leads to oppression, which drives them to repentance and restoration. A key theme is **divine justice balanced with compassion** - God allows enemies to oppress Israel as righteous punishment, yet "his soul was grieved for the misery of Israel" (v. 16).

The commentary stresses **the futility of idolatry**. Henry notes Israel's descent from serving multiple gods to completely forsaking the Lord: "Those that think to serve both God and Mammon will soon come entirely to forsake God... If God have not all the heart, he will soon have none of it."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons. Regarding **contentment**, he observes about Jair's sons: "Villages are cities to a contented mind." On **leadership**, he notes how Tola "humbled" himself to serve, demonstrating that "those that humble themselves shall be exalted."

The commentary warns against **compromise with worldly influences**. Israel's adoption of foreign gods made them "mean and despicable" and economically dependent on other nations. Henry applies the principle: "In quo quis peccat, in eo punitur—Wherein a person offends, therein he shall be punished."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

For **verses 1-5**, Henry notes that "quiet and peaceable reigns, though the best to live in, are the worst to write of." Tola arose to "defend Israel" by reforming abuses and healing wounds from Abimelech's usurpation.

Regarding **verse 6**, Henry emphasizes the comprehensive nature of Israel's idolatry, serving gods "of Syria, Zidon, Moab, Ammon, and the Philistines" while completely forsaking the Lord.

On **verses 13-14**, Henry highlights God's ironic challenge: "Go and cry unto the gods which ye have chosen; let them deliver you."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to other biblical texts. He references **Genesis 49:14-15** regarding Issachar's servant nature, **Deuteronomy 13:12** on God's commands against idolatrous cities, and **Leviticus 26:17, 37** on promised judgments for disobedience.

He connects to **1 Samuel 8:1-3** regarding Samuel's sons as judges, **Joshua 22** concerning the altar controversy, and **Ezekiel 16:3** describing Israel as "perfect Amorites" due to their degeneration.

The commentary demonstrates Henry's theological depth in showing how individual passages fit within Scripture's broader narrative of God's covenant faithfulness despite human unfaithfulness.
