# Matthew Henry Commentary on Judges 18: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**The Spread of Idolatry and Its Consequences**
Henry traces how "a little fire kindle[s]" a great matter, showing idolatry's progression from Micah's household to the entire tribe of Dan. The commentary emphasizes how spiritual corruption spreads when there is no proper spiritual authority - highlighted by the recurring phrase "there was no king in Israel."

**Divine Providence and Human Responsibility**
Henry notes the tension between God's sovereignty and human accountability. While the Danites received their inheritance by divine allotment, their methods (theft, idolatry, violence) violated God's law. He observes their "carelessness and regardlessness of God and his providence" in consulting false oracles rather than seeking God properly at Shiloh.

**The Danger of Spiritual Compromise**
The commentary critiques how the Danites "thought God, who had forbidden the religious use of graven images, would yet own them in consulting an image." This represents the broader theme of syncretistic worship that plagued Israel during the judges period.

## Key Practical Applications

**The Importance of Good Governance**
Henry provides extensive commentary on verse 7, explaining magistrates as "heirs of restraint" whose duty is suppressing evil. He warns that communities without proper authority become vulnerable: "how miserable, and how near to ruin, those places are that either have no magistrates or none that bear the sword to any purpose."

**Against Spiritual Laziness**
Using the Danites' initial reluctance to claim their inheritance, Henry applies this spiritually: "Heaven is a very good land, where there is no want of any thing; our God has, by the promise, given it into our hands; let us not then be slothful in making it sure."

**The Value of Personal Initiative**
He commends the principle that "it is better to have a little of one's own than always to hang upon others," encouraging self-reliance over dependency.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 6 - False Assurance:**
Henry notes the irony of the priest's blessing: "Go in peace, before the LORD is your way," observing how the Levite "humoured the thing" regardless of whether he believed it, giving false spiritual comfort.

**Verse 7 - Societal Vulnerability:**
The detailed analysis of Laish reveals both moral and practical dangers: bad governance led to wickedness, while false security led to military vulnerability. "The more secure always the less safe."

**Verses 9-10 - Spiritual Application:**
Henry transforms the spies' encouragement into evangelistic language about heaven, drawing parallels between earthly and spiritual inheritance.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to several biblical themes:
- **Genesis 49:17** and **Deuteronomy 33:22** - Dan's character as having "subtlety of a serpent" and courage of "a lion's whelp"
- **Psalm 12:8** - "the wicked walk on every side" when there's no proper authority
- **Ezekiel 14:3** - questioning whether God should be consulted by idolaters
- **Joshua 19:47** - the original allotment of Laish to Dan

The commentary consistently emphasizes how this narrative illustrates the broader spiritual decline during the judges period, when "every man did that which was right in his own eyes." Henry sees this chapter as both historical record and spiritual warning about the consequences of abandoning proper worship and governance.
