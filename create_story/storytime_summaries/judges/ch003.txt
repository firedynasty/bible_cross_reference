# Matthew Henry Commentary on Judges 3: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents God's sovereignty as the central theme, showing how divine wisdom operates even through Israel's enemies. He emphasizes that God "wisely permitted" the Canaanites to remain—not merely as punishment, but for Israel's "real advantage." This reflects God's ability to work all things for good, even using opposition to strengthen His people.

The cyclical pattern of sin, judgment, repentance, and deliverance dominates the chapter. Henry notes how Israel's spiritual compromises inevitably led to physical bondage, demonstrating that "when they inclined to worship other gods they forgot the Lord their God." He draws parallels to the church militant, writing that "Israel was a figure of the church militant, that must fight its way to a triumphant state."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry emphasizes the danger of spiritual compromise, particularly through unequal marriages and cultural accommodation. He warns that in mixed marriages "there is more reason to fear that the bad will corrupt the good than to hope that the good will reform the bad." 

The commentary stresses the value of hardship in spiritual development. God left enemies among Israel so they might "learn war" and avoid becoming soft through prosperity. Henry applies this to Christians: "The soldiers of Christ must endure hardness" and "corruption is therefore left remaining in the hearts even of good Christians, that they may learn war."

He highlights the importance of proper methodology in leadership, noting how Othniel "first judged Israel, reproved them, called them to account for their sins, and reformed them, and then went out to war. This was the right method."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On verses 1-4, Henry explains God's dual purpose: testing Israel's faithfulness while training them for warfare. He notes that their country's richness required periodic hardship to prevent "luxury and effeminacy."

Regarding verse 6's intermarriage, Henry observes that Israel "could not advance either their honour or their estate" through such unions, but would "mar their blood instead of mending it."

For Othniel's calling (verse 10), Henry emphasizes that "the Spirit of the Lord came upon him"—showing divine commission rather than human appointment.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this chapter to broader biblical themes. He references Jeremiah 12:9 about God's heritage being "a speckled bird," and Jeremiah 12:5 about running with footmen to contend with horses. He cites 1 Corinthians 11:19 regarding necessary heresies to reveal the faithful, and 2 Timothy 2:3 about soldiers of Christ enduring hardness.

The commentary links Othniel's proper methodology to Isaiah 33:22, noting that Christ must be "our Judge and Law-giver" before He can save us. Henry also connects the pattern to Psalm 78, showing how affliction drives people to earnest prayer who previously "would scarcely speak to him."

Henry's treatment reveals how Judges 3 illustrates timeless principles of divine discipline, spiritual warfare, and the necessity of maintaining separation from worldly compromise while trusting God's sovereignty through trials.
