# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Judges 14: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Samson as "a riddle, a paradox of a man" who accomplished divine purposes through seemingly improper means. The central theological theme is **divine providence working through imperfect human choices**. Henry emphasizes that Samson's marriage to a Philistine woman "was of the LORD" (v. 4), designed to create "an occasion against the Philistines."

Most significantly, Henry interprets **Samson as a type of Christ**—one "designed not to be a pattern to us (who must walk by rule, not by example), but a type of him who, though he knew no sin, was made sin for us." Just as Christ assumed human nature to defeat spiritual enemies, Samson joined "in affinity" with Israel's enemies to destroy them.

The **lion encounter symbolizes spiritual victory**: "Our Lord Jesus having conquered Satan, that roaring lion, believers find honey in the carcase, abundant strength and satisfaction...from that victory."

## Key Practical Applications

**On Marriage and Parental Authority**: Henry strongly emphasizes children's duty to seek parental consent before marriage. Those who marry without parents' approval "wilfully unchild themselves." He warns against being "guided only by his eye, and governed by his fancy" in choosing a spouse, noting "he that in the choice of a wife is guided only by his eye...must afterwards thank himself if he find a Philistine in his arms."

**On Humility**: Samson's decision not to boast about killing the lion demonstrates that "modesty and humility make up the brightest crown of great performances."

**On Sharing Blessings**: Samson sharing honey with his parents illustrates how "children should be grateful to their parents" and believers should "communicate their experience to their friends and relations."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 4**: Henry explains this wasn't merely God overruling events, but God actively moving Samson's heart to make this choice for strategic purposes against the Philistines.

**Verses 5-6**: The lion encounter served to reveal Samson's strength to himself, as "many decline doing the service they might do because they know not their own strength."

**Verses 8-9**: The honey in the lion's carcase becomes a powerful metaphor—"Honey is honey still, though in a dead lion"—teaching that God's gifts retain their value regardless of their unlikely sources.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects Samson's story extensively to other biblical narratives:
- **Romans 8:3**: Christ appearing "in the likeness of sinful flesh"
- **Genesis 6:2**: Warning against unequal marriages from the antediluvian period  
- **Deuteronomy 7:3**: God's prohibition against marrying devoted nations
- **Luke 2:51**: Christ's subjection to parents paralleling Samson's obedience
- **1 Samuel 17:36**: David's preparation through fighting wild beasts
- **Psalm 118:12**: Being compassed about like bees
- **Matthew 4:1**: Christ's encounter with Satan, the roaring lion

Henry masterfully weaves these connections to show Samson's role in salvation history while drawing practical lessons for Christian living.
