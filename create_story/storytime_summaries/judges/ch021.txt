# Matthew Henry on Judges 21: Mercy in the Aftermath of Divine Judgment

## Main Theological Themes

Matthew Henry presents Judges 21 as a sobering study in the complexity of divine justice and human mercy. The primary theological tension centers on Israel's struggle between righteous zeal and compassionate restoration. Henry emphasizes that while their initial anger against Benjamin's sin was justified, their subsequent grief over the tribe's near-extinction reveals God's heart for restoration even after severe judgment.

The commentary highlights how "there may be over-doing in well-doing" and warns that zealous passion often creates the need for repentance. Henry notes that "what we say and do in a heat our calmer thoughts commonly wish undone again," illustrating how even righteous anger must be governed by wisdom and mercy.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several crucial applications for Christian living. First, he emphasizes that "great care must be taken in the government of our zeal, lest that which seemed supernatural in its causes prove unnatural in its effects." This warns against allowing righteous indignation to become destructive excess.

Second, he teaches that "even necessary justice is to be done with compassion," noting that "God does not punish with delight, nor should men." This principle guides believers toward tempering justice with mercy in their relationships and conflicts.

Henry also observes that true Israelites don't "aim to raise themselves upon the ruins of their neighbours," but rather work toward restoration and healing of broken relationships.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Regarding verses 2-3, Henry notes how Israel brought their grief to "the house of God" because "thither they brought all their doubts, all their counsels, all their cares, and all their sorrows." Their lament over having "one tribe lacking" reflects their understanding of God's covenant design for the twelve tribes.

On their oath-making (v. 1), Henry explains their marriage prohibition arose because they "judged those unworthy to match with a daughter of Israel that had been so very barbarous and abusive to one of the tender sex." Yet this created their later dilemma when seeking restoration.

The Jabesh-Gilead solution (vv. 8-14) demonstrates how "men are commonly more zealous to support their own authority than God's," as they executed their own curse while neglecting to drive out the Jebusites whom God had commanded them to destroy.

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects Israel's grief to 2 Corinthians 7:10-11, distinguishing between godly sorrow that leads to salvation and mere regret over consequences. He references Numbers 31:17-18 regarding the precedent for sparing virgins in warfare, and Leviticus 27:29 concerning devoted things.

The commentary links to 1 Samuel 11:4, suggesting that Saul's later concern for Jabesh-Gilead stemmed from this alliance between Benjamin and that city. Henry also draws parallels to 2 Corinthians 2:7 regarding the need to forgive and comfort those who repent.

The chapter concludes with the ominous refrain that "every man did that which was right in his own eyes," which Henry presents as the root cause of this entire tragic sequence—a period when Israel lacked proper spiritual authority and guidance, leading to both the original sin and the excessive response to it.
