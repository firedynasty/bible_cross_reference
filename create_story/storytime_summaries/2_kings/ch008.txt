# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 8: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **divine sovereignty in judgment and providence** throughout the chapter. He notes that "when God judgeth he will overcome" - if lesser judgments fail to bring repentance, God sends greater ones. The seven-year famine demonstrates that God's judgments "are at his beck, and will come when he calls for them."

A key theme is **the distinction between Israel and other nations**. Henry observes that the famine was peculiar to Israel while neighboring countries had plenty, showing "the immediate hand of God" and that "the sins of Israel...were more provoking to God than the sins of their neighbours, because of their profession of relation to God" (referencing Amos 3:2).

The chapter also illustrates **God's faithfulness in rewarding righteousness**. The Shunammite woman's earlier kindness to Elisha is rewarded through prophetic warning and providential restoration of her property.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

- **The wisdom of heeding prophetic warnings**: "It is our happiness to foresee an evil, and our wisdom, when we foresee it, to hide ourselves." The Shunammite's early departure allowed her to settle elsewhere "upon such good terms" before the crowd.

- **The uncertainty of earthly relationships**: Henry notes how the woman who once "dwelt so securely among her own people" later found "her own familiar friends...proved so unjust and unkind." This demonstrates "such uncertainty there is in the creature that that may fail us which we most depend upon."

- **The responsibility of authorities**: "It is not enough for those in authority that they do no wrong themselves, but they must support the right of those that are wronged."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-6 (The Shunammite's restoration)**: Henry praises the king's accessibility to his subjects and notes how Providence ordered the "happy coincidence" of Gehazi's testimony with the woman's petition, making "the king ready to believe" and "ready to grant her request."

**Verses 7-15 (Hazael and Ben-hadad)**: Henry finds it remarkable that the Syrian king consulted Elisha, noting "God sometimes fetches to himself that honour from strangers which is denied him...by his own professing people." He contrasts this with the king of Israel who had consulted "the god of Ekron" instead.

**Verse 13 (Hazael's protest)**: Henry observes that Hazael's question "What, is thy servant a dog?" shows how "men's corruptions...sleep in them as fire in the flax" until circumstances awaken them.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry makes several biblical connections:

- **Amos 3:2** - "You only have I known, therefore will I punish you" (explaining Israel's special judgment)
- **Joel 1:3-4** - Suggesting this famine involved locusts and caterpillars
- **1 Kings 19:15** - Connecting Elisha's visit to Damascus with God's earlier command to anoint Hazael
- **Psalms 82:3-4 and Proverbs 31:9** - Supporting the king's duty to give redress to the wronged
- **Proverbs 25:19 and Micah 7:5** - On the difficulty of finding trustworthy people in times of trouble

Henry masterfully weaves these narratives together to demonstrate God's sovereignty, justice, and faithfulness while drawing practical wisdom for believers facing similar challenges of trust, authority, and divine providence.
