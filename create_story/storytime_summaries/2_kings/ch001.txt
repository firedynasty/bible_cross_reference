# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings Chapter 1: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes the stark contrast between divine authority and human rebellion in this chapter. The central theological theme is **God's sovereignty over earthly rulers**, demonstrated through "how great the prophet looks and how little the prince." He interprets Ahaziah's consultation with Baal-zebub as representing "practical and constructive atheism," arguing that when people seek answers from false sources, they effectively declare "there is no God at all in Israel."

The commentary presents God's judgment as both **providential and prophetic** - working through circumstances (Moab's rebellion, Ahaziah's fall) and through His word (Elijah's pronouncements). Henry sees the fire from heaven as divine vindication of prophetic authority, noting that "God was always ready to plead their cause, and avenge the injuries done to them."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**On seeking divine guidance**: He criticizes Ahaziah's question "Shall I recover?" as foolish, arguing "We should be more thoughtful what will become of us after death than how, or when, or where, we shall die, and more desirous to be told how we may conduct ourselves well in our sickness."

**On the consequences of rebellion**: "When he rebelled against God, and revolted from his allegiance to him, Moab rebelled against Israel." Henry teaches that personal unfaithfulness to God often results in others' unfaithfulness to us - "If men break their covenants with us... we must reflect upon our breach of covenant with God."

**On false security**: Despite being in his palace, Ahaziah wasn't safe from divine judgment: "A man's house is his castle, but not to secure him against the judgments of God... He is never safe that has God for his enemy."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 2** - Henry sees Ahaziah's fall as providential judgment: "The cracked lattice is as fatal to the son, when God pleases to make it so, as the bow drawn at a venture was to the father."

**Verses 3-4** - He interprets the rhetorical question about God in Israel as exposing the king's practical atheism: consulting foreign gods implies "you think there is no God at all in Israel."

**Verses 9-12** - On Elijah calling down fire, Henry notes this wasn't for self-defense "but to prove his mission, and to reveal the wrath of God from heaven." He emphasizes the captain's contradiction in calling Elijah "man of God" while trying to arrest him.

**Verses 13-15** - The third captain's humble approach demonstrates that "submission and humility" open the way for mercy.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this narrative to several biblical themes:

- **Job 18:9-10** - on how "the snare is laid for the sinner in the ground where he thinks least of it"
- **Matthew 12:24** - linking Baal-zebub to Beelzebub in the New Testament, noting "the gods of the Gentiles were devils"
- **Matthew 3:4** - comparing Elijah's simple dress to John the Baptist, "the Elias of the New Testament"
- **Zechariah 1:6** - on the enduring power of God's word across generations
- **Luke 9:54** - noting that while Elijah acted "by divine impulse," Jesus wouldn't allow disciples to follow this precedent

Henry concludes by emphasizing divine justice fulfilled: Ahaziah "died according to the word of the LORD which Elijah had spoken," demonstrating that God's warnings, whether heeded or ignored, ultimately prove true.
