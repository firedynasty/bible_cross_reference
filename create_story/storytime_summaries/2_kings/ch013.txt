# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 2 Kings Chapter 13

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as a study in divine mercy despite persistent rebellion. The central theological tension is between God's righteous judgment against idolatry and His covenant faithfulness. Henry emphasizes that "there is forgiveness with him, that he may be feared," highlighting God's readiness to show mercy even when people have "forsaken him." 

The commentary explores how "the debauching of a nation will certainly be the debasing of it," demonstrating the inevitable consequences of spiritual compromise. Yet Henry notes God's swift response to prayer, showing how He is "willing to find out a reason to be gracious" even looking back to "that ancient covenant which Israel had so often broken."

## Key Practical Applications

**On National Decline**: Henry warns that abandoning true worship leads to national weakness, observing how Israel's "crown [was] profaned and its honour laid in the dust." The reduction of Israel's military to merely "fifty horsemen, ten chariots, and 10,000 footmen" illustrates how spiritual corruption produces practical vulnerability.

**On Prayer**: He emphasizes that "it becomes kings to be beggars at God's door, and the greatest of men to be humble petitioners at the footstool of his throne. Need will drive them to it."

**On Honoring Faithful Ministers**: Henry commends King Joash's respect for Elisha, noting that "one good action shall make a better figure in God's book than twenty great ones," and it brings "a much better reputation to honour a prophet than to conquer a king."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-6**: Henry contrasts Israel's former glory with their current shame, highlighting the persistent calf worship that "levelled themselves with the nations that worshipped the work of their own hands."

**Verses 14-19**: On Elisha's final days, Henry notes the prophet "lived long" (about 60 years of ministry) but spent his latter years in relative obscurity. He observes that "the time of his flourishing was less than the time of his living," encouraging contentment with retirement in old age.

Henry finds significance in King Joash using Elijah's own words ("My father, my father, the chariot of Israel") to mourn Elisha, noting: "Those that give just honours to the generation that goes before them are often recompensed with the like from the generation that comes after them."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects God's mercy to **Leviticus 26:42** regarding God's remembrance of His covenant with Abraham. He references **Deuteronomy 4:7** about Israel's privilege of having "God nigh unto them." He also cites **Proverbs 11:25** regarding the principle that "he that watereth...shall be watered also himself."

The commentary emphasizes how God's grace transcends individual servants: "God's grace is not tied to one hand. He can bury" his servants while continuing His work through others, demonstrating the continuity of divine purpose beyond any single prophet or leader.
