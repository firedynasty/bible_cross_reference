# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 25: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry frames the destruction of Jerusalem as the inevitable consequence of persistent sin and rebellion against God. He emphasizes that Jerusalem's former glory rested on two foundations: as "the royal city, where were set 'the thrones of the house of David'" and as "the holy city, where was the testimony of Israel"—both now departed. The commentary repeatedly stresses divine judgment: "When God judges he will overcome" and "Those that by sin have provoked God to leave them will find that innumerable evils will compass them about."

The destruction of Solomon's temple receives particular theological attention. Henry notes the irony that this house, which "had the eye and heart of God perpetually upon it," was not "fire-proof against God's judgments." He explains this as God showing "how little he cares for the external pomp of his worship when the life and power of religion are neglected."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several moral lessons from Jerusalem's fall. He warns against trusting in external religious forms: "The people trusted to the temple, as if that would protect them in their sins...but God, by this, let them know that when they had profaned it they would find it but a refuge of lies."

He also highlights how divine providence sometimes reverses social fortunes: "When the rich Jews, who had been oppressive to the poor, were made strangers...the poor whom they had despised and oppressed had liberty and peace in their own country. Thus Providence sometimes remarkably humbles the proud and favours those of low degree."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides vivid details about the siege's progression, noting how the eighteen-month siege fulfilled multiple prophecies. He explains how two seemingly contradictory prophecies about Zedekiah were both fulfilled—Jeremiah said he would go to Babylon, while Ezekiel said he wouldn't see it. Both proved true when Zedekiah's eyes were put out before his transport.

The commentary emphasizes the month-long delay between the city's capture and its destruction as a final period of divine patience: "This space God gave them to repent...but in vain; their hearts (for aught that appears) were still hardened."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects the narrative to other biblical passages, citing Lamentations 1:1 ("Thus has Jerusalem become as a widow"), Ezekiel's prophecies about famine and judgment, Jeremiah's warnings, and the Psalms. He notes the remarkable historical parallel that both temples were destroyed on the same date—the first by Chaldeans, the second by Romans—demonstrating divine sovereignty over history.

The commentary concludes by pointing forward to redemption, suggesting that God's promises about the temple "must be understood of the gospel-temple, which is God's rest for ever."
