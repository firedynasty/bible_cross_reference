## Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 19 Summary

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents 2 Kings 19 as a powerful demonstration of God's sovereignty over earthly kingdoms and His faithfulness to those who trust Him. The central theological theme is divine deliverance "not by sword or bow, but by prayer and prophecy, and by the hand of an angel." 

Henry emphasizes that God's honor is at stake when blasphemers like Sennacherib mock Him, making this a divine quarrel rather than merely a human conflict. The commentary highlights God as "the living God" in contrast to dead idols, showing His unique ability to hear, respond, and act decisively for His people.

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several vital applications for believers facing overwhelming circumstances:

**Prayer as Primary Response**: When Hezekiah faced crisis, "his greatest relief is that he has a God to go to." Rather than calling a war council, the king went to the house of the Lord, demonstrating that "when the church's enemies are very daring and threatening it is the wisdom and duty of the church's friends to apply to God."

**Seeking Intercessory Prayer**: Henry notes the importance of requesting others' prayers while not neglecting personal prayer. He observes that "those who speak from God to us we should in a particular manner desire to speak to God for us."

**Proper Response to Blasphemy**: Hezekiah's sackcloth and mourning over God's dishonor shows that "great men must not think it any disparagement to them to sympathize with the injured honour of the great God."

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry commends Hezekiah's immediate turn to mourning and prayer, noting that "royal robes are not too good to be rent, nor royal flesh too good to be clothed with sackcloth" when God's honor is at stake.

**Verse 3**: The metaphor "children are come to the birth, and there is not strength to bring forth" represents complete helplessness - the critical moment has arrived but human strength has failed.

**Verses 6-7**: God's response through Isaiah shows His personal investment: "They have blasphemed me." Henry emphasizes that God's "short threatening" would accomplish more than "all the impotent menaces that came from Rabshakeh's mouth."

**Verses 14-19**: Hezekiah's prayer demonstrates proper approach to God - acknowledging His absolute sovereignty ("thou art the God, even thou alone"), presenting the crisis honestly, and seeking deliverance for God's glory rather than personal comfort.

### Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout his commentary:
- Psalm 73:17 (finding understanding in God's sanctuary)
- 2 Chronicles 32:3 (Hezekiah's earlier counsels)
- Joel 2:17 (priests praying in trouble)
- Genesis 20:7 (prophets as intercessors)
- Hosea 13:13 (the birth metaphor)
- Psalm 74:22 ("Arise O God! plead thy own cause")

The commentary presents this chapter as a masterpiece of divine intervention, where "God glorified himself and saved his people" through the faith and prayers of His servants.
