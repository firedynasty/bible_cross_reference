# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 5: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Naaman's healing as a powerful type of Gentile inclusion in God's grace, noting that "our Saviour's miracles were intended for the lost sheep of the house of Israel, yet one, like a crumb, fell from the table to a woman of Canaan; so this one miracle Elisha wrought for Naaman, a Syrian; for God does good to all, and will have all men to be saved." He draws a striking parallel between Naaman's cleansing representing the calling of the Gentiles and Gehazi's punishment typifying "the blinding and rejecting of the Jews, who envied God's grace to the Gentiles."

The commentary emphasizes God's sovereignty over all nations, observing that even Syria's military victories came from the Lord: "The preservation and prosperity even of those that do not know God and serve him must be ascribed to him, for he is the Saviour of all men, but especially of those that believe."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons about human nature and spiritual truth. Regarding Naaman's greatness despite his leprosy, he notes: "No man's greatness, or honour, or interest, or valour, or victory, can set him out of the reach of the sorest calamities of human life" and "Every man has some but or other in his character, something that blemishes and diminishes him." He memorably quotes Bishop Hall: "the basest slave in Syria would not change skins with him."

The commentary praises the servant girl's faithfulness, showing how "servants may be blessings to the families where they are, by telling what they know of the glory of God and the honour of his prophets." Henry also warns against misinterpretation: "We often create a great deal of uneasiness to ourselves by misinterpreting the words and actions of others that are well intended."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry emphasizes how Naaman didn't despise the maid's counsel despite her lowly status, asking: "O that those who are spiritually diseased would hearken thus readily to the tidings brought them of the great Physician!" He notes Naaman's honorable approach - going personally rather than sending for Elisha, traveling openly with gifts and royal letters.

Regarding Elisha's indirect response, Henry suggests the prophet either "had retired, at this time, for devotion" or "knew Naaman to be a proud man, and he would let him know that before the great God all men stand upon the same level."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this account to Luke 4:27 where Jesus references Naaman's healing. He links the scattered servant girl to Acts 8:4 regarding the dispersion spreading God's knowledge. The commentary references Psalm 8:2 about children declaring God's works and Jeremiah 29:7 about seeking peace in captivity. Daniel 5:19 is cited regarding human versus divine authority over life and death.

The narrative demonstrates how "Those that would be cleansed from the spiritual leprosy must wait at Wisdom's gate," connecting to broader biblical themes of humble obedience and divine grace transcending ethnic boundaries.
