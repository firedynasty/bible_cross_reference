# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 10: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes divine sovereignty and judgment throughout this chapter. He repeatedly stresses that "there shall fall unto the earth nothing of the word of the LORD" (v. 10), demonstrating God's faithfulness in executing promised judgment against Ahab's house through Elijah's prophecy. The commentary presents these violent events as divine justice rather than mere political upheaval.

Henry also explores the theme of righteous zeal versus incomplete obedience. While praising Jehu's destruction of Baal worship, he notes the tragic flaw: Jehu "retained" Israel's golden calf worship, leading to continued divine judgment through Hazael's attacks.

## Key Practical Applications

The commentary warns against misplaced trust in earthly powers. When Samaria's leaders submit to Jehu, Henry observes: "Learn hence not to trust in a friend nor to put confidence in a guide not governed by conscience." He argues that those "false to God" will prove unfaithful to earthly masters.

Henry draws a powerful lesson about divine authority: "Much more reason may we thus argue ourselves into a subjection to the great God. Many kings and great men have fallen before his wrath...how then shall we stand? We must either bend or break."

He also warns against arbitrary power, noting how Jezebel's forced compliance in Naboth's murder created the same servile spirit that later betrayed Ahab's sons.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-10**: Henry highlights the irony that Ahab's "seventy sons" couldn't preserve his house from divine vengeance, commenting that "numerous families, if vicious, must not expect to be long prosperous."

**Verses 15-16**: The encounter with Jehonadab demonstrates the importance of righteous alliances. Henry notes Jehu's question "Is thine heart right, as my heart is with thy heart?" as crucial for spiritual partnership.

**Verses 18-28**: Henry praises Jehu's strategic deception in destroying Baal worship, seeing it as justified by its righteous purpose. However, he warns this "bloody work...is not now, in any case, to be drawn into a precedent."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this narrative to broader biblical themes through several references:
- **Jeremiah 48:10** - against doing God's work "deceitfully, or by halves"
- **Isaiah 34:5-6** - these destructions as "types of the final destruction of all the ungodly"
- **Judges 8:30** - comparing Ahab's seventy sons to Gideon's numerous offspring
- **Jeremiah 35** - referencing Jehonadab's descendants' faithfulness to his commands
- **1 Kings 21:11** - connecting the elders' submission to their earlier complicity in Naboth's murder

Henry concludes by noting the incomplete nature of Jehu's reformation, showing how partial obedience brings partial blessing, setting up the pattern of Israel's continued decline.
