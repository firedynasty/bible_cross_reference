# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Kings 3: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Sovereignty and Providence**: Henry emphasizes God's overruling providence even when human folly creates difficulties. He notes how "God suffers his people, by their own improvidence, to bring themselves into distress, that the wisdom, power, and goodness of his providence may be glorified in their relief."

**Incomplete Reformation**: A central theme is the danger of partial obedience. Henry critiques Jehoram's superficial reforms, observing that "Those do not truly, nor acceptably, repent or reform, who only part with the sins that they lose by, but continue their affection to the sins that they get by." Though Jehoram removed Baal worship, he maintained Jeroboam's golden calves for political convenience.

**The Consequences of Unequal Alliances**: Henry warns against partnerships with unbelievers, noting that Jehoshaphat's alliance with Ahab's house was problematic: "There is nothing got by being yoked with unbelievers."

## Key Practical Applications

**The Importance of Seeking God Early**: Henry emphasizes consulting God before making major decisions rather than waiting for crisis. Regarding Jehoshaphat's late inquiry: "It was well that Jehoshaphat enquired of the Lord now, but it would have been much better if he had done it sooner."

**Humble Service Leads to Greatness**: Using Elisha's example of serving Elijah, Henry teaches: "He that will be great, let him learn to minister: he that will rise high, let him begin low."

**God's Gracious Provision**: Henry marvels at God's care: "It would often be bad with us if God did not take more care of us, both for soul and body, than we take for ourselves."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3**: Henry sees Jehoram as "bad but not so bad" as his parents, yet warns that partial reformation "did not prevent the destruction of Ahab's family."

**Verses 11-12**: He highlights the irony that a servant knew of Elisha's presence while the kings remained ignorant, showing how "Elisha made himself known, not to the kings" but to God-fearing servants.

**Verse 13**: Elisha's confrontation with Jehoram demonstrates prophetic boldness in challenging hypocritical rulers who seek God only in distress.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry draws extensive scriptural parallels: He connects the wilderness water crisis to Numbers 20:2 and Israel's earlier wilderness wanderings. He references Solomon's wisdom from Proverbs 27:23-24 regarding the temporary nature of wealth. The theme of human foolishness blaming God is supported by Proverbs 19:3. He also alludes to Ecclesiastes 7:17 about being "overmuch wicked" and connects Elisha's humble service to Christ's teaching about greatness through service.

Henry concludes that while "the house of Ahab is doomed to destruction," God's mercy still operates through His prophets, demonstrating divine patience even with imperfect rulers and their misguided alliances.
