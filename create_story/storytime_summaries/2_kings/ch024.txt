# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 24: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **divine sovereignty over human affairs**, showing how God orchestrates political events to accomplish His purposes. He notes that "many are serving God's purposes who are not aware of it," demonstrating how even pagan rulers like Nebuchadnezzar fulfill divine judgment. The commentary highlights **intergenerational consequences of sin**, particularly how "the sins of Manasseh" were visited upon later generations, illustrating that "time will not wear out the guilt of sin and that reprieves are not pardons."

A central theme is **the certainty of God's word**. Henry emphasizes that the invasion occurred "according to the word of the LORD, which he spoke by his servants the prophets," declaring that "rather shall heaven and earth pass away, than any word of God fall to the ground."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws sobering lessons about **the consequences of rebellion against divine authority**. He observes that "If Jehoiakim had served his God as he should have done, he would not have been servant to the king of Babylon," teaching that disobedience to God leads to earthly subjugation.

The commentary warns about **the persistent nature of guilt**, particularly regarding "innocent blood" that God "would not pardon" temporally, showing "what a provoking sin murder is, how loud it cries, and how long." Henry also emphasizes how nations must "lament the sins of their fathers, lest they smart for them."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: Henry explains Jehoiakim's three-year servitude as divine discipline, noting that rebellion only brought "more trouble."

**Verses 2-4**: The coalition of enemies (Chaldeans, Syrians, Moabites, Ammonites) represents God's orchestrated judgment, with Henry emphasizing twice that this came "at the commandment of the LORD."

**Verses 8-16**: Regarding Jehoiachin's brief reign, Henry poignantly observes: "He came to the crown, not to have the honour of wearing it, but the shame of losing it." The young king's immediate surrender showed he lacked "the faith and piety of an Israelite" and therefore "had not the resolution of a man, of a soldier, of a prince."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects the events to prophetic fulfillment, citing **Isaiah 20:17** regarding temple treasures being carried to Babylon, and **Deuteronomy 28:49** about God bringing "a nation against thee from far." He references **Jeremiah 22:18-19** concerning Jehoiakim's dishonorable burial "with the burial of an ass," and **Jeremiah 22:30** about Jehoiachin being "written childless."

The commentary also connects to **2 Chronicles 12:8** about learning "the difference between his service and the service of the kings of the countries," and **Deuteronomy 32:34** regarding God's stored judgments.

Henry concludes by showing how these events demonstrate that "threatenings will be fulfilled as certainly as promises, if the sinner's repentance prevent not," establishing the chapter as a sobering testimony to divine justice and the reliability of God's word.
