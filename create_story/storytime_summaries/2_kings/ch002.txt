# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Kings 2: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Elijah's translation as a profound theological contrast, noting "men are as their end is" - comparing the wicked king's disgraceful departure with the holy prophet's glorious exit. He interprets Elijah's translation typologically, seeing it as prefiguring Christ's ascension and "the opening of the kingdom of heaven to all believers."

The commentary emphasizes God's sovereignty in translation, suggesting three divine purposes: (1) rewarding Elijah's extraordinary service and encouraging faithful witness, (2) providing proof of eternal life during a spiritually dark period, and (3) foreshadowing the evangelical dispensation. Henry notes that God honored one who "knew sin, and yet never tasted death" as an encouragement that "if we have our conversation in heaven, while we are here on earth, we shall be there shortly."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from Elisha's devotion: believers should maximize spiritual fellowship while time permits, declaring "we should do all the spiritual good we can one to another...while we are together, because we are to be together but a little while." He warns against spiritual fatigue: "Let not those that follow Christ come short by tiring at last."

The commentary emphasizes preparation for loss, noting that we should "carefully do the duty of every relation, that we may reflect upon it with comfort when it comes to be dissolved." Henry also highlights the importance of spiritual mentorship and succession in ministry, as seen in the schools of prophets that maintained faith during apostasy.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On Elisha's persistent following (vv. 2-6), Henry suggests Elijah's attempts to leave him behind were tests of faithfulness, "like Naomi's persuading Ruth to go back." Elisha's response "I will not leave thee" demonstrated both love and spiritual hunger for edification.

Regarding the sons of prophets' knowledge (vv. 3,5), Henry observes their recognition that "The Lord will take them; we lose them not till he calls for them." The Jordan crossing (v. 8) parallels Israel's entrance to Canaan, with Elijah's mantle replacing the ark as a token of God's presence, symbolizing how "the death of Christ has divided those waters" for believers.

The double portion request (v. 9) Henry interprets not as double Elijah's power, but double that of other prophets - appropriate for one expected to lead the prophetic schools.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to broader biblical themes. He references Joshua 3:7 regarding Jordan's division, 2 Peter 1:15 on leaving testimonies for those remaining, John 16:6 concerning sorrow at departure, and Zechariah 2:13 about reverent silence. The translation parallels Enoch's experience and prefigures the transformation of living believers at Christ's return, while the chariot of fire connects to divine manifestations throughout scripture.
