# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Kings 15: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Justice and Patience**: Henry emphasizes God's faithful fulfillment of His promise to Jehu—that his descendants would reign "unto the fourth generation"—while simultaneously demonstrating divine justice through Israel's mounting chaos. He observes that "God had tried the people of Israel both with judgments and mercies," yet they remained "impenitent and unreformed."

**Pride and Divine Humbling**: Through Azariah's leprosy, Henry illustrates that "if great men be proud men, some way or other God will humble them," emphasizing that even kings are subject to divine discipline when they overstep their boundaries.

**Consequences of National Sin**: The commentary presents Israel's political instability as divine judgment, noting that "for the transgression of the land, many were the princes thereof." Henry sees the succession of murderous kings as evidence that God's patience has limits.

## Key Practical Applications

**The Danger of Spiritual Compromise**: Henry repeatedly criticizes the failure to remove high places, demonstrating how partial obedience undermines spiritual progress. Even good kings like Azariah lacked "zeal and courage enough to take away the high places."

**Political Stability and Unity**: Henry draws practical lessons about governance, observing that "nothing does more towards the making of a nation an easy prey to a common enemy than intestine broils and contests for the sovereignty. Happy the land where that is settled."

**Ambition's Destructive Power**: He warns that "the ambition of the great men made the nation miserable," showing how personal ambition destroys national welfare.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Azariah's Leprosy (v. 5)**: Henry explains this as punishment for "presumptuous invasion of the priests' office," noting that though likely repented, "for warning to others, he was continued under this mark of God's displeasure as long as he lived."

**Menahem's Cruelty (v. 16)**: The brutal treatment of Tiphsah exemplifies how "one wicked man is often made a scourge to another, and every wicked man, at length, a ruin to himself."

**The Pattern of Evil (v. 9, 18, 24, 28)**: Henry observes that despite political turmoil, "all these kings did that which was evil in the sight of the Lord, for they walked in the sins of Jeroboam." Though "their government was so often altered, yet never for the better."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this chapter to **2 Chronicles 26:16** for fuller details of Azariah's presumptuous act and its consequences. He references **Leviticus 26:21** regarding escalating divine judgment: "If you will yet walk contrary to me, I will punish you yet seven times more."

He cites **Hosea 1:4** concerning God's eventual judgment on Jehu's house and **Hosea 5:7** ("Now shall a month devour them") as prophetic commentary on Shallum's brief reign. The principle that "he resisteth the proud" reflects **James 4:6**.

Henry concludes by noting this period immediately preceded Israel's final captivity, suggesting that "if they had, in these days of confusion and perplexity, humbled themselves before God and sought his face, that final destruction might have been prevented." The commentary serves as both historical analysis and spiritual warning about the consequences of persistent rebellion against divine authority.
