# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel Chapter 11: Summary

## **Main Theological Themes and Interpretations**

Henry treats David's fall as a sobering demonstration of human frailty, emphasizing that "the best of men" can fall when "God leaves them to themselves." He frames this narrative as divinely permitted instruction, noting it "was written for our learning" so "that he that thinks he stands may take heed lest he fall." The commentary stresses that even Scripture's greatest heroes are faithfully recorded in their failures, demonstrating the Bible's sincere historical integrity rather than partisan hagiography.

The overarching theme is God's sovereignty in allowing this fall to reveal "what was in his heart," paralleling Hezekiah's testing. Henry sees Providence working even through Uriah's noble refusal to go home, ensuring David's sin would be exposed rather than concealed.

## **Key Practical Applications**

Henry draws several pointed applications from David's downfall:

**Spiritual vigilance is essential**: David's idleness and neglect of duty created the pathway to temptation. "When we are out of the way of our duty we are in the way of temptation." Henry emphasizes that "Standing waters gather filth" and "The bed of sloth often proves the bed of lust."

**The progression of sin**: Henry traces how "the way of sin is down-hill" - from idle curiosity to inquiry to action. He warns that "The beginning of lust, as of strife, is like the letting forth of water; it is therefore wisdom to leave it off before it be meddled with."

**Leadership accountability**: David's position as king made his sin doubly heinous since he was entrusted with executing justice on others. "With what face could he rebuke or punish that in others which he was conscious to himself of being guilty of?"

## **Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights**

**Verse 1**: David's absence from battle was the critical first step. Henry notes that David personally led the Syrian campaign but delegated this one, putting himself "out of the way of his duty" and "in the way of temptation."

**Verse 2**: The "evening-tide" arising from bed reveals David's spiritual sloth. Henry observes David "had dozed away the afternoon in idleness" and likely omitted his customary noon prayers.

**Verses 3-4**: Henry traces the fatal progression from seeing to inquiring to acting, noting how "lust immediately conceived" despite David learning Bathsheba was married.

**Verse 11**: Uriah's noble response contrasts sharply with David's behavior. Henry praises Uriah's resolution: "While the army was encamped in the field, he would not lie at ease in his own house."

## **Cross-References to Other Scripture**

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout: Job 31:11 on adultery's heinousness, Romans 2:22 on hypocrisy in leadership, Jeremiah 5:8 on habitual adultery, 2 Chronicles 32:31 on Hezekiah's testing, Proverbs 6:34 on jealousy's rage, and Habakkuk 2:15-16 on making others drunk. He references Jesus' prayer "lead us not into temptation" as the daily necessity this chapter illustrates.

Henry concludes that while this story has "emboldened" some to sin, making it "a savour of death unto death," for others it serves as "a savour of life unto life," awakening them to "holy jealousy" and "constant watchfulness against sin."
