# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel 8 - Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes that David's conquests come after "seeking first the kingdom of God," having properly established the ark. The key theological principle is that "all other things were added to him" once spiritual priorities were set. Henry sees these victories as **typological of Christ's victories**: "All David's victories were typical of the success of the gospel against the kingdom of Satan, in which the Son of David rode forth, conquering and to conquer."

The commentary stresses divine providence throughout, noting "The LORD preserved David whithersoever he went." Henry interprets David's conquests as fulfilling God's ancient promises to Abraham regarding Israel's territorial inheritance extending to the Euphrates River.

## Key Practical Applications

**Spiritual Priorities**: David's success flows from first establishing proper worship and relationship with God. Henry draws the parallel that when believers prioritize God's kingdom, material blessings follow.

**Stewardship of Victory**: David's dedication of all spoils to God (v. 11-12) exemplifies proper stewardship. Henry notes this "crowned all his victories" and distinguished David from worldly conquerors like "Alexander's or Caesar's" who sought personal glory.

**Justice in Leadership**: David's administration demonstrates that external victories mean nothing without internal justice. Henry emphasizes David "executed judgment and justice unto all his people" with impartiality and accessibility to "even the meanest" citizens.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 2** (Moabite judgment): Henry explains the "measuring with lines" as dividing the population into thirds—two parts condemned, one spared. He notes significantly that "the line that was to keep alive...is ordered to be a full line"—emphasizing mercy's extension "in favorem vitae—so as to favour life."

**Verse 4** (Destroying horses): Henry connects David's hamstringing of horses to obedience to Deuteronomy 17:16's prohibition against multiplying horses, showing David "placed his strength not in chariots nor horses, but in the living God."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive scriptural connections throughout: Judges 13:5 (Samson beginning Philistine deliverance), Numbers 24:17 (Balaam's prophecy about smiting Moab), Genesis 15:18 (Abrahamic land promises), Psalm 60 (David's victory psalm), Luke 11:22 (strong man disarmed), and Revelation 21:24 (nations bringing glory into New Jerusalem).

The commentary concludes with prophetic significance: David's dual role of conquest and justice preparation foreshadows Christ, "who doth in righteousness both judge and make war" (Revelation 19:11).
