# Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Samuel Chapter 20

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry opens with the sobering observation that "the clouds return after the rain," illustrating how David's troubles continue to multiply as fulfillment of the prophetic warning that "the sword should never depart from his house." He interprets Sheba's rebellion as part of the "hereditary enmity" against God's anointed, drawing parallels to opposition against the Messiah's kingdom, noting that "he that sits in heaven laughs at them all" (Psalm 2:1-2).

The commentary presents this chapter as demonstrating the consequences of sin, particularly David's polygamy, which "proved a grief and shame to him." Henry sees divine justice operating throughout these events, from the rebellion's timing to David's family disgrace.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed lessons for leadership and Christian living:

**On Impartiality**: "It is as impolitic for princes to be partial in their attentions to their subjects as it is for parents to be so to their children; both should carry it with an even hand."

**On Conflict Resolution**: "The beginning of strife is as the letting forth of water; it is therefore wisdom to leave it off before it be meddled with" (Proverbs 17:14).

**On Human Fickleness**: People tend toward extremes - "Today Hosanna, to-morrow Crucify" - as demonstrated by Israel's rapid shift from claiming "ten parts in David" to declaring "we have no part in him."

**On Loyalty**: Henry critiques fair-weather allegiance, noting that "most love a loyalty, as well as a religion, that is cheap and easy."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry identifies Sheba as "a man of Belial" from Saul's tribe, representing persistent opposition to God's chosen king. The rebellion arose from David's apparent favoritism toward Judah during reconciliation.

**Verse 3**: David's treatment of his concubines reflects both duty (putting away the defiled), prudence (avoiding public shame), and justice (punishing their "easy submission to Absalom's lust").

**Verses 8-10**: Henry provides detailed analysis of Joab's calculated murder of Amasa, emphasizing its treacherous nature - done "subtilely," "treacherously," "impudently," and "unseasonably" when unity was crucial against a common enemy.

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects this chapter to broader scriptural themes:
- Links Sheba's rebellion to Psalm 2:1-2 regarding opposition to the Messiah
- References Proverbs 17:14 on the dangers of strife
- Cites 1 Corinthians 5:1 regarding things that "ought not to be so much as named"
- Draws parallels between opposition to David and hostility toward Christ's kingdom

The commentary emphasizes that earthly honors are "slippery" and "subject to continual mutability," while celebrating that God's favor involves "no shadow of change." Henry concludes that this narrative demonstrates both the consequences of sin and the ultimate triumph of God's purposes despite human rebellion and treachery.
