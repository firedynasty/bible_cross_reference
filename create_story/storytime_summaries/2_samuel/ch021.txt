# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel 21: Summary

## Main Theological Themes

Henry emphasizes **divine justice and covenant faithfulness** as central themes. He notes that "God's judgments often look a great way back," demonstrating that "time does not wear out the guilt of sin" and "there is no statute of limitation to be pleaded against God's demands." The three-year famine represents God's delayed but certain judgment for Saul's violation of Israel's sacred oath to the Gibeonites.

The commentary stresses **corporate responsibility**, explaining that while individuals shouldn't normally suffer for others' sins, this was an "altogether extraordinary" case where "God had made himself an immediate party to the cause." Henry warns that "guilt and a curse are a bad entail upon a family," showing how covenant-breaking affects future generations.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**On seeking God in trials**: "When we are under God's judgments we ought to enquire into the grounds of the controversy." He critiques David's delayed response, noting "even good men are often slack and remiss in doing their duty."

**On honoring commitments**: The Gibeonites' situation demonstrates that "the despising of the oath, and breaking of the covenant, will be recompensed." Sacred promises, even those "obtained by a wile," must be honored.

**On restitution**: Henry emphasizes that "money is no satisfaction for blood" and "in vain do we expect mercy from God, unless we do justice upon our sins."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On **verse 1** (the famine inquiry): Henry observes that David was "slow in his enquiries" but "God was ready in his answer," teaching patience in seeking divine guidance.

Regarding **verses 4-6** (the Gibeonites' demands): Henry praises their refusal of monetary compensation, noting they "would not seem weary of the service" despite having opportunity to escape their servitude.

On **verse 7** (sparing Mephibosheth): Henry commends David for ensuring "that while he was avenging the breach of one oath, he might not himself break another."

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry extensively references the original Gibeonite covenant in **Joshua 9**, noting how their "stratagem" still resulted in a binding oath. He connects this to **Numbers 35:31-33** regarding blood money and **Genesis 9:6** on blood requiring blood.

The commentary links Saul's violation to his earlier clemency toward Amalekites, suggesting he sought "to atone for his clemency" through misplaced zeal. Henry also references **1 Chronicles** to establish the chronological placement of these events.

## Memorable Phrases

Henry's most striking observations include his warning that covenant-breaking brings divine retribution "though it may be long first," and his sobering conclusion: "Better that seven of Saul's bloody house be hanged than that all Israel be famished." This reflects his understanding that sometimes severe justice serves the greater good of God's covenant people.
