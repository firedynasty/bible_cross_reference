# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel 5: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents David's coronation as king over all Israel as a **typological fulfillment pointing to Christ's kingdom**. He emphasizes that "his kingdom typified that of the Messiah, which was to come to its height by degrees," noting that "we see not yet all things put under him (Heb. ii. 8), but we shall see it." The commentary draws parallels between David's gradual rise and Christ's progressive reign.

The **divine appointment theme** runs throughout, with Henry noting that God's sovereignty orchestrates events. David's establishment came because "he was to be a type of Christ, with whom God's hand should be established." The principle that **"former good services to the public"** deserve greater trust reflects God's pattern of promoting the faithful.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers and leaders:

**For those accepting Christ as King**: "Those who take Christ for their king may thus plead with him: 'We are thy bone and thy flesh, thou hast made thyself in all things like unto thy brethren (Heb. ii. 17)'—therefore be our ruler."

**For leaders**: Princes should "feed their people as shepherds, in every thing consulting the subjects' benefit, feeding them and not fleecing them." David's example shows leaders should serve for their people's welfare, not personal aggrandizement.

**For spiritual growth**: Henry emphasizes recognizing God's favor: "Many have the favour of God and do not perceive it, and so want the comfort of it." David "perceived that the LORD had established him king" and understood it was "for his people Israel's sake."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3**: The tribes' threefold argument for David's kingship—blood relation, proven service, and divine appointment—provides the strongest foundation, with Henry noting divine calling as "the greatest inducement of all."

**Verses 6-10**: Henry interprets the Jebusites' "blind and lame" taunt as either referring to their idol-gods ("they have eyes and see not, feet and walk not") or their overconfidence in fortifications. David's conquest represents Christ's victory: "The Jebusites, Christ's enemies, must first be conquered and dispossessed...then Christ divides the spoil, sets up his throne there."

**Verse 12**: Henry emphasizes David's spiritual perception and humility in recognizing God's favor and attributing his success to serving others rather than self-aggrandizement.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive biblical connections throughout: Deuteronomy 17:15 (qualifications for kingship), Hebrews 2:17 (Christ's identification with humanity), Isaiah 60:10 (strangers building God's walls), Psalm 89:21-28 (God's covenant with David), 1 Chronicles 11:6 and 12:23-40 (parallel accounts), and Revelation 21 (the new Jerusalem). He also references Numbers 4:3 and Luke 3:23 regarding the significance of beginning ministry at thirty years old.

The commentary consistently connects Old Testament events to New Testament fulfillment, making David's reign a clear prophetic type of Christ's eternal kingdom.
