# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 2 Samuel Chapter 10

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as a study in divine justice and human character. The primary theological theme centers on God's sovereignty in warfare and judgment - as Henry notes, "none ever hardened his heart against God and prospered." He emphasizes that those who oppose God's anointed king (David as a type of Christ) inevitably face divine retribution. The narrative demonstrates how "sinners daringly provoke God, and expose themselves to his wrath, and never consider that he is stronger than they."

Henry also highlights the principle of reaping what one sows, noting that David's reputation advanced "for gratitude, in returning kindness, and for justice, in repaying injuries."

## Key Practical Applications

**On Responding to Kindness:** Henry emphasizes that gratitude should motivate continued generosity: "the pleasure of doing one kind and generous action should excite us to another." David's desire to show kindness to Hanun for his father's sake exemplifies this principle.

**On Dealing with False Accusations:** When facing unjust reproaches, Henry counsels patience: "Let us learn not to lay too much to heart unjust reproaches; after awhile they will wear off of themselves...the injured reputation in a little time grows again, as these beards did."

**On Mutual Support:** From Joab's military strategy, Henry draws lessons about Christian fellowship: "Christ's soldiers should thus strengthen one another's hands in their spiritual warfare. The strong must succour and help the weak."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2:** Henry commends David's gratitude, noting he "is not nice in examining the grounds and principles" of past kindness received, but simply seeks to return it graciously.

**Verses 3-4:** On the Ammonites' suspicion, Henry observes that "False men are ready to think others as false as themselves" and quotes Bishop Patrick: "there is nothing so well meant but it may be ill interpreted...by men who love nobody but themselves."

**Verses 11-12:** Henry particularly praises Joab's pre-battle speech, highlighting the phrase "Be of good courage and let us play the men for our people, and for the cities of our God" as exemplifying proper motivation for conflict - defending both community and divine honor.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this narrative to several biblical passages:

- **1 Samuel 11:2** - Referencing Nahash's previous cruelty to Jabesh-Gilead
- **Deuteronomy 23:3** - Noting restrictions on Ammonites entering God's congregation  
- **1 Corinthians 10:22** - Comparing Ammonite provocation to sinners challenging God's strength
- **Psalm 37:6-7** - Encouraging patience when facing false accusations
- **Luke 22:32** - Applying Joab's mutual support strategy to strengthening fellow believers

Henry masterfully weaves together historical narrative, practical wisdom, and spiritual application, demonstrating how even accounts of ancient warfare provide timeless lessons about character, justice, and trust in divine providence.
