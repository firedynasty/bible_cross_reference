# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel 18: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry interprets this chapter through the lens of divine justice and mercy, seeing Absalom's rebellion as representative of humanity's wickedness toward God, while David's paternal love reflects God's mercy toward rebellious mankind. He emphasizes that **"what it is to take counsel against the Lord and his anointed"** ultimately leads to destruction, as God fights against rebels despite their apparent advantages.

The commentary highlights God's providence in keeping David from the battlefield, noting that divine wisdom orchestrated events so David's tenderness wouldn't interfere with God's determined judgment on Absalom. Henry sees Absalom's unusual death as divinely appointed punishment, hanging **"between heaven and earth, as unworthy of either, as abandoned of both."**

## Key Practical Applications

**Leadership and Counsel**: Henry praises David's willingness to accept wise counsel from subordinates, noting **"It is no piece of wisdom to be stiff in our resolutions, but to be willing to hear reason, even from our inferiors."** This demonstrates humility in leadership and the value of prudent advisors.

**Parental Love vs. Justice**: The commentary addresses the tension between natural affection and necessary justice. While acknowledging David's paternal love, Henry warns against **"injurious tenderness"** and **"bloody indulgence"** that can enable wrongdoing rather than correct it.

**Military Wisdom**: Henry emphasizes that **"Good order and good conduct may sometimes be as serviceable in an army as great numbers,"** teaching believers to maximize their resources through wisdom rather than relying solely on strength.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-5**: Henry notes the strategic wisdom in David's army organization and his willingness to be overruled by wise counsel. David's charge to **"Deal gently for my sake with the young man, even with Absalom"** demonstrates extraordinary mercy toward a rebellious son.

**Verses 6-8**: The defeat in Ephraim's wood shows divine judgment, where **"the wood devoured more people that day than the sword devoured,"** indicating God's active involvement in fighting against the rebels.

**Verses 9-18**: Absalom's death by hanging in the oak represents poetic justice - his pride (possibly his hair) becomes his snare. Henry notes the mule abandoning its burden, symbolizing creation's desire to be freed from corruption.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects David's mercy toward Absalom to Christ's prayer for His persecutors: **"Father, forgive them,"** suggesting this prefigures **"that immeasurable mercy of the true King and Redeemer of Israel."**

He references **Romans 8:21-22** regarding creation groaning under corruption's burden, **Psalm 52:6** about the righteous seeing God's judgment, **Jeremiah 48:44** about inescapable divine justice, and **Numbers 16:29-30** comparing Absalom's unusual death to God's judgment on Dathan and Abiram.

The commentary also cites **Proverbs 30:17** as a warning against filial disobedience, making Absalom's fate **"in terrorem—to frighten children from disobedience to their parents."**

Henry concludes that this civil war victory brought no triumph, as **"all these that were slain were his own subjects, and the common interest of his kingdom was weakened by the slaughter,"** reflecting the tragic cost of internal rebellion against rightful authority.
