# Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Samuel 13

## Main Theological Themes

Henry interprets this chapter as the direct fulfillment of divine judgment upon David's house, noting that "The righteous God had lately told David...that he would 'raise up evil against him out of his own house.'" The commentary emphasizes divine justice in action - David's sins of adultery and murder are now reflected in his children's crimes of incest and murder. Henry observes that "grace does not run in the blood, but corruption does," highlighting how parental sin can influence children while noting that "Godly parents have often been afflicted with wicked children."

## Key Practical Applications

**Parental Influence**: Henry warns that "Parents know not how fatal the consequences may be if in any instance they give their children bad examples." He notes David's children imitated his "false steps" rather than his devotion.

**Friendship and Counsel**: The commentary strongly condemns Jonadab's role, stating "The case of those is very miserable whose friends, instead of admonishing and reproving them, flatter them and forward them in their sinful ways." True friends should redirect from sin, not enable it.

**Beauty as Temptation**: Henry warns that "Beauty is a snare to many" and advises that "Those that are peculiarly handsome have no reason, on that account, to be proud, but great reason to stand upon their watch."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry traces "the steps of Amnon's sin" systematically: First, demonic influence plants unnatural lust (vv. 1-2), noting that "Fleshly lusts are their own punishment...and are the rottenness of the bones." Second, Jonadab's subtle counsel enables the plot (vv. 3-5), with Henry observing Jonadab's twisted logic about princely privilege: "Quicquid libet licet--Your will is law." Third, the deceptive execution involving David's unwitting participation (vv. 6-11).

Regarding Tamar's response, Henry praises her arguments as showing "nothing could be said more pertinently, nor with greater strength of argument," highlighting her appeals to family relationship, divine law, and social consequence.

## Cross-References

Henry connects this narrative to broader scriptural themes, referencing Job 33:20 on appetite loss, Psalm 10:8-10 on predatory behavior, Leviticus 18:9 and 20:17 on incest prohibitions, and Proverbs 31:13 on virtuous women's industriousness. He also draws parallels to Jezebel's enabling of Ahab's sin (1 Kings 21:7).

## Conclusion

Henry presents this chapter as both divine judgment and moral instruction, demonstrating how unchecked sin spreads through families and how evil counsel accelerates moral decline. The commentary serves as a warning about parental influence and the devastating consequences of abandoning godly principles.
