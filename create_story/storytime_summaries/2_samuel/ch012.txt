# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Samuel Chapter 12 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry frames this chapter as the progression from conviction to repentance, emphasizing God's pursuing grace: "Though God may suffer his people to fall into sin, he will not suffer them to lie still in it." The theological centerpiece is that God sends restoration before David seeks it—"He sends after us before we seek after him, else we should certainly be lost."

The commentary highlights the severity of sin as fundamentally "despising the commandment of the Lord," showing that sin's core malignity lies in "making light of the divine law and the law-maker." Henry interprets David's spiritual condition during the nine months before Nathan's confrontation as suspended communion with God: "his harp was out of tune, and his soul like a tree in winter, that has life in the root only."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from Nathan's approach, emphasizing the need for "prudence in giving reproofs" through the parable method. He notes that Nathan didn't avoid confronting David despite his royal status, demonstrating that faithful ministers must speak God's word "whether it speak terror or comfort."

The commentary warns against the progression of evil desire, citing Jewish interpretation of the "traveler" metaphor: "in the beginning it is only so, but, in time, it becomes a guest, and, in conclusion, is master of the house." Henry also addresses the inadequacy of polygamy as remedy for lust, noting that "when once the law of unity is transgressed, the indulged lust will hardly stint itself."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On Nathan's parable strategy (vv. 1-4), Henry explains how the rich man's taking the poor man's lamb represented David's abuse of power and luxury. The "wayfaring man" symbolizes evil imagination that, while initially transient in David's heart, led to devastating consequences.

Regarding David's harsh judgment (vv. 5-6), Henry attributes the severity to David's "present roughness of temper, being under guilt, and not having himself as yet received mercy."

The confrontation "Thou art the man" (v. 7) demonstrates Nathan's faithfulness, speaking "as an ambassador from the great God, with whom is no respect of persons." Henry emphasizes God's past generosity to David as making his ingratitude more grievous.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects David's restoration prayer to Psalm 51:12, 15 ("Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation, and open thou my lips"). He references Isaiah 57:17-18 regarding God's healing intervention for the wayward heart.

The commentary cites Exodus 22:1 for the fourfold restitution law, Proverbs 5:19 on marital contentment, and Proverbs 6:30-32 comparing theft and adultery. He also alludes to 2 Thessalonians 3:15 regarding proper correction of erring believers and Isaiah 50:7 on prophetic boldness.

The chapter concludes with Henry noting how Solomon's birth and Rabbah's conquest demonstrate that "God did not deal with David according to his sins," showing divine mercy alongside discipline.
