# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 2 Samuel 23

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Inspiration and Covenant Theology**: Henry emphasizes David's divine inspiration, noting that "The Spirit of God spoke by him" and "holy men spoke and wrote as they were moved by the Holy Ghost." This validates the Psalms' authority in Christian devotion. Central to the passage is the **everlasting covenant**, which Henry interprets as both the Davidic covenant and, more significantly, the covenant of grace made with all believers through Christ.

**Typological Interpretation**: Henry sees David's words as having dual application—historically to David and his royal line, but prophetically to **Christ as the ultimate Son of David**. The description of just rule "as the light of the morning" points to Christ as "the light of the world" and "the branch of the Lord."

## Key Practical Applications

**For Leaders and Magistrates**: Henry draws clear leadership principles from verses 3-4: rulers "must be just, ruling in the fear of God." He emphasizes they rule "over men—not over beasts" and must remember they serve "under God, and for him." Good governance brings blessing like "light of the morning" and "tender grass."

**For Believers Facing Family Struggles**: David's confession "Although my house be not so with God" offers comfort to godly parents whose children disappoint. Henry notes that "the children of godly parents are often neither so holy nor so happy as might be expected" because "it is corruption, not grace, that runs in the blood."

**For Personal Devotion**: David's testimony about the everlasting covenant being "all my salvation, and all my desire" provides a model of faith. Henry encourages believers to say: "Let me have an interest in this covenant...and I have enough, I desire no more."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: David is described by both "the meanness of his original" (son of Jesse) and "the height of his elevation" (anointed king and "sweet psalmist of Israel"). Henry notes it's "true preferment to be serviceable to the church in acts of devotion."

**Verses 3-4**: The requirement for just rule "in the fear of God" applies both to David's successors and prophetically to Christ's righteous reign.

**Verse 5**: This becomes the heart of David's testament—his confidence in God's "everlasting covenant, ordered in all things, and sure." Henry calls this covenant "well ordered" because "whatever is required in the covenant is promised."

**Verses 6-7**: The "sons of Belial" as thorns represents both a warning to magistrates about dealing with wickedness and the ultimate fate of the wicked.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects these verses extensively to other biblical passages: **Psalm 72** (also David's "last words" about righteous rule), **Isaiah 11:1-5 and 32:1-2** (Messianic prophecies), **Isaiah 55:3** ("sure mercies of David"), **Hebrews 3:3** (Christ's house), and **Isaiah 53:10-12** (covenant with the suffering Servant). He also references **2 Samuel 7:18** for David's similar meditation on God's covenant promises.

The commentary masterfully weaves together covenant theology, Christological interpretation, and practical wisdom for both leaders and believers facing family disappointments, all anchored in David's confidence in God's unchanging promises.
