# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel Chapter 4 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents divine providence as the central theme, showing how God orchestrates David's rise to power through human actions—even evil ones—while maintaining His sovereignty. The commentary emphasizes God's justice through human instruments, as David serves as "the chief magistrate" and "avenger of blood" by office. Henry highlights the tension between God's ultimate control and human moral responsibility, noting that while Providence brought about Ish-bosheth's removal, the murderers remained fully culpable for their treacherous act.

The commentary also explores the theme of divine protection, referencing Psalm 91:12 about angels bearing us up in their arms, and God's faithfulness in David's declaration that "the Lord...hath redeemed my soul out of all adversity."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed moral lessons. He warns against slothfulness, using Ish-bosheth's midday sleeping as an example: "Love not sleep, lest thou come to poverty and ruin. The idle soul is an easy prey to the destroyer." When difficulties should invigorate us, becoming dispirited "betrays both our crowns and lives."

He condemns using evil means for supposedly good ends, particularly critiquing those who "think to serve the interests of the Son of David by any immoral practices." Henry warns that such people "shall not escape the damnation of hell," emphasizing that "Christianity was not intended to destroy humanity."

The commentary advocates for charitable judgment, noting David's recognition of Ish-bosheth as "a righteous person" despite their conflict, teaching us "to make the best, not only of our friends, but of our enemies."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: Henry interprets Ish-bosheth's "feeble hands" as complete dependence on Abner's support, leaving him spiritually broken after Abner's death.

**Verses 2-3**: He provides detailed geographical context about the Beerothites' migration to Gittaim, connecting it to broader tribal movements and the city's strategic location between the rocks Bozez and Seneh.

**Verse 4**: Mephibosheth's injury becomes a meditation on childhood vulnerability and divine protection, showing how "even the children of princes...are not always safe."

**Verses 5-7**: Henry contrasts Ish-bosheth's slothfulness with the murderers' treachery, noting how "the king's corn-chamber and his bed-chamber lay near together" in the simplicity of those times.

**Verse 11**: David's characterization of Ish-bosheth as righteous demonstrates gracious judgment toward enemies.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout his commentary. He references **Joshua 18:25** and **Joshua 9:17** for geographical context about Beeroth and the Gibeonites. **Nehemiah 11:33** supports his observations about Gittaim's later prominence.

For theological support, he cites **Psalm 91:12** regarding divine protection in infancy, and **1 Samuel 25:38-39** concerning God's sovereign justice versus human vengeance. He draws a parallel with Jehu's false zeal, and references the law forbidding hanging whole bodies.

The commentary concludes with an implicit reference to persecution of early Christians, alluding to those who "hate their brethren, and cast them out, and say, Let the Lord be glorified," demonstrating Henry's application of biblical principles to contemporary religious conflicts.

Henry's treatment reveals 2 Samuel 4 as a complex study in providence, justice, and the proper means of advancing God's kingdom through righteous rather than expedient methods.
