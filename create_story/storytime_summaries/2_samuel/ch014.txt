# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel 14: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry interprets this chapter as demonstrating "the folly of David in sparing [Absalom] and indulging him in his wickedness," which would soon lead to severe consequences through Absalom's rebellion. The central theological tension revolves around **divine mercy versus justice**. The wise woman's parable draws parallels between God's merciful nature and David's role as king, noting that "God devises means, that his banished be not expelled from him" (v. 14).

Henry emphasizes God's pattern of mercy, referencing how "the Lord thy God spared Cain, who slew his brother" and "forgave thee the blood of Uriah." This establishes a theological principle: "Nothing is more proper, nor more powerful, to engage us to every duty, especially to all acts of mercy and kindness, than to remember the Lord our God."

## Key Practical Applications

**For Leaders and Judges**: Magistrates should serve as "patrons of oppressed widows" and show compassion to the afflicted. Henry notes David's readiness "to comfort the mourners, especially the mourning widows," reflecting God's character as "Judge of the widows" (Psalm 68:5).

**For Parents**: Henry observes that "Parents are not easy, unless their children be safe," highlighting natural parental concern for children's welfare in both temporal and eternal matters.

**For Showing Mercy**: The commentary advocates for mercy over strict justice when circumstances warrant, suggesting that sometimes "mercy should rejoice against judgment," especially when considering God's own merciful dealings with sinners.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: Joab "perceived that the king's heart was toward Absalom," showing political astuteness in reading David's true feelings despite official banishment.

**Verse 14**: Henry highlights the profound theological statement that "God devises means, that his banished be not expelled from him," seeing this as a reflection of divine mercy toward humanity.

**Verses 11-12**: The woman's request that David "remember the LORD thy God" serves as both an appeal for an oath and a reminder of God's merciful character that should influence royal judgment.

## Cross-References and Scriptural Connections

Henry connects this passage to **Nathan's parable** (2 Samuel 12), noting similar methodology in confronting David indirectly. He references **Genesis 4:15** regarding God's protection of Cain, **Genesis 27:45** concerning Rebekah's concern for both sons, and **Hebrews 6:17-18** about God's unchangeable promises.

Most significantly, Henry draws a parallel to **Luke 21:16-18**, where "the Son of David has assured all that put themselves under his protection that, though they should be put to death for his sake, not a hair of their head shall perish."

The commentary presents Joab's stratagem as politically shrewd but ultimately contributing to David's unwise leniency that would have devastating consequences for his kingdom.
