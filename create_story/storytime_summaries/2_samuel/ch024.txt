# Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Samuel 24

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as David's final struggle with sin despite his spiritual maturity, showing that even godly leaders can fall into pride. The central theological tension involves God's sovereignty and Satan's temptation working together - Henry explains that "Satan, as an enemy, suggested it for a sin...God, as righteous Judge, permitted it" to punish Israel for other sins while teaching that "princes' sins affect entire kingdoms."

The commentary emphasizes divine mercy amid judgment, noting how "God repents of the judgment and David thereby made more penitent," demonstrating God's willingness to stay His hand when genuine repentance occurs.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons: First, leaders must guard against pride, as "it was folly for him to be proud of the numbers of his people, when they were God's people, not his." Second, believers should welcome faithful reproof, noting how Joab's respectful challenge showed "it would be good for us to have a friend that would faithfully admonish us when we say or do any thing proud or vain-glorious."

The commentary warns that "many things, not in themselves sinful, turn into sin to us by our inordinately delighting in them," emphasizing the heart's motives over external actions.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Regarding the nature of David's sin, Henry offers multiple interpretations but concludes "the worst thing in numbering the people was that David did it in the pride of his heart" - showing "proud conceit of his own greatness" and "proud confidence in his own strength."

On David's repentance (v.10), Henry notes that "his heart smote him before the prophet came to him," highlighting how "true penitents, whose consciences are tender and well informed, see that evil in sin which others do not see."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this account to 1 Chronicles 21:1 (Satan's provocation), Exodus 30:12 (the half-shekel requirement), and Proverbs 6 (God's hatred of pride). He parallels David's situation with Hezekiah's pride in showing his treasures and relates the principle of leaders' sins affecting nations to the earlier famine caused by Saul's sin.

The commentary concludes with David's altar-building as a token of reconciliation, showing how genuine repentance leads to restored worship and fellowship with God.
