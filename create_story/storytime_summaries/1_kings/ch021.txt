# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Kings 21: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Ahab's coveting of Naboth's vineyard as a powerful illustration of how "inordinate desires expose men to continual vexations." The central theme revolves around the destructive nature of discontent and covetousness. Henry notes that "discontent is a sin that is its own punishment and makes men torment themselves," observing how Ahab had "all the delights of Canaan...and yet all this avails him nothing without Naboth's vineyard."

The commentary emphasizes God's sovereignty over the land of Israel, explaining that "Canaan was in a peculiar manner God's land; the Israelites were his tenants" with divine restrictions on land alienation. Henry portrays the conflict between human authority and divine law, noting how Naboth "would rather hazard the king's displeasure than offend God."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons about contentment and godly living. He contrasts Paul's contentment in prison with "Ahab discontent in a palace," teaching that contentment "arises not from the condition, but from the mind." 

Regarding marriage relationships, he notes the danger of ungodly counsel, showing how Jezebel "feeds his pride and passion, and blows the coals of his corruptions." He warns that leaders are "hurried apace towards their ruin" when they have advisors who "stir them up to acts of tyranny."

Henry also addresses the abuse of religious forms for wicked purposes, observing that "there is no wickedness so vile, so horrid, but religion has sometimes been made a cloak and cover for it" when describing Jezebel's false fast.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On Naboth's refusal (v.3), Henry suggests Naboth may have viewed "his earthly inheritance as an earnest of his lot in the heavenly Canaan," and was likely "one of the 7000 that had not bowed the knee to Baal."

Regarding Jezebel's question "Dost thou now govern the kingdom of Israel?" (v.7), Henry provides both good and bad interpretations - either a reasonable call to rise above petty concerns, or a dangerous encouragement toward tyrannical abuse of power.

The commentary notes the supreme wickedness of Jezebel's plot, calling her orders to the magistrates an "impudent defiance to every thing that is just and sacred" that combined false religion with perverted justice.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry references the tenth commandment ("Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house") and Leviticus 25:28 regarding jubilee year land restrictions. He cites Proverbs 6:26 about the adulteress hunting "for the precious life" and connects to 2 Kings 9:34's description of Jezebel as "that cursed woman."

The commentary also alludes to the 7,000 who hadn't bowed to Baal and contrasts Ahab's discontent with Paul's prison contentment, demonstrating how Scripture interprets Scripture in Henry's methodology.
