# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Kings 12: Summary

## Main Theological Themes

Henry emphasizes divine sovereignty working through human folly. The kingdom's division was "from the LORD" (v. 15) to fulfill prophecy, demonstrating how "God serves his own wise and righteous purposes by the imprudences and iniquities of men." He notes the tragic irony that Solomon, despite his wisdom and thousand wives, produced only one heir—"a fool"—observing that "sin is a bad way of building up a family" and "neither wisdom nor grace runs in the blood."

The commentary highlights the consequences of abandoning godly counsel. Henry emphasizes that "the way to rule is to serve," contrasting the humble wisdom of older counselors with the pride-driven advice of young companions.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed lessons about leadership and counsel. He warns young people about choosing advisors, noting the danger of relying on those who "feed their pride, gratify their vanity, and further them in their pleasures" rather than offering wise guidance. He observes that "great wits have not always the most wisdom; nor are those to be relied on as our best friends that know how to make us merry, for that will not make us happy."

Regarding the people's complaints about taxation, Henry notes their spiritual blindness: "They complain not of his father's idolatry and revolt from God; that which was the greatest grievance of all was none to them." This reveals misplaced priorities—caring more about money than spiritual matters.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On Rehoboam's foolish response (vv. 10-11), Henry criticizes the young counselors' flashy but empty advice: "My little finger shall be thicker than my father's loins." He comments, "That is not always the best sense that is best worded."

Henry identifies four fatal errors in Rehoboam's approach: validating complaints against Solomon, overestimating his own abilities, threatening harsh rule, and provoking "a people that by long ease and prosperity were made wealthy, and strong, and proud."

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry references Hosea 4:10 regarding Solomon's many wives yet few heirs, and connects the Shechem location to Judges 9 (Abimelech's rebellion) and Joshua 24:1 (covenant renewal), calling the location choice "ominous." He alludes to 1 Samuel 8's warning about kingship's burdens, noting the people "were told before that this would be the manner of the king and yet they would have one."

Henry concludes with the sobering observation that both kingdoms were weakened: "Thus did Judah become weak, being deserted by their brethren, and Israel, by deserting the house of the Lord." The division represents both political folly and spiritual apostasy, showing how pride and poor counsel can destroy what took generations to build.
