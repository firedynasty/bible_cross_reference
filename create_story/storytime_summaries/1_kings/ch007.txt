# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Kings Chapter 7

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes the principle of **seeking God first** throughout this chapter. He notes that Solomon "began with the temple, built for God first, and then all his other buildings were comfortable," connecting this to Matthew 6:33. This establishes the foundational theme that **"the surest foundations of lasting prosperity are those which are laid in an early piety."**

The commentary highlights **divine wisdom working through human gifts**, particularly in God's use of Hiram, who combined "the ingenuity of a Tyrian, and the affection of an Israelite." Henry observes that "God will serve himself by the common gifts of the children of men," showing how God employs diverse talents for His purposes.

The magnificence of the temple furnishings reflects the principle that **"God, who is the best, must be served and honoured with the best."** This theological emphasis on offering excellence to God permeates Henry's analysis of the costly materials and skilled workmanship.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for Christian living:

**Priority in Building:** Solomon spent seven years on the temple but thirteen on his own house, demonstrating that "he was in no haste for his own palace, but impatient till the temple was finished." The application: "Thus we ought to prefer God's honour before our own ease and satisfaction."

**Contentment with Simple Living:** Regarding Solomon's country retreat, Henry notes that "those whose lot is cast in the country may be well reconciled to a country life" since even great princes found their "most pleasant days" in "country retirements."

**Excellence in Service:** The use of finest materials teaches that believers should offer their best to God in worship and service.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides fascinating details about Solomon's building projects. The "house of the forest of Lebanon" was likely an actual country residence in Lebanon itself, made possible by Solomon's extensive chariot system. The structure featured "three tiers of windows on each side, light against light," creating "prospect against prospect" for magnificent views.

The judicial system is highlighted in verse 7's "porch of judgment," where Solomon personally heard appeals, establishing the principle of accessible royal justice.

Regarding Hiram's mixed heritage (v. 14), Henry sees providential design: "If he had the ingenuity of a Tyrian, and the affection of an Israelite...it was happy that the blood of the two nations mixed in him."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects Solomon's building priorities to **Matthew 6:33** ("seek first the kingdom of God"), establishing the New Testament parallel to Old Testament priorities.

He references **Proverbs 9:1** regarding Wisdom's house with seven pillars, relating it to Solomon's porches that provided shelter for those waiting for audience.

The temple's construction recalls **1 Chronicles 29:2**, where David prepared materials, showing continuity between father and son's devotion.

Henry draws parallels between the temple courts and Solomon's personal courts (v. 12), noting how "so well did he like the model of God's courts that he made his own by it."

**Historical Context:** Henry notes that "as the tabernacle was built with the wealth of Egypt, so the temple with the wit of Tyre," showing God's sovereignty over nations in accomplishing His purposes.

The commentary concludes by emphasizing that these detailed descriptions serve ongoing spiritual instruction, demonstrating how material excellence in worship reflects spiritual priorities and God's worthiness of humanity's finest offerings.
