## Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Kings 20

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **God's sovereignty in judgment and mercy**, showing how divine favor can extend even to wayward rulers when it serves God's purposes. The commentary highlights how **sin removes divine protection** - Ahab's apostasy made Israel vulnerable to Syrian attacks. Henry notes that these victories came during a period when "God favoured the nation with successes...that, if possible, they might be led to repentance by the goodness of God." The overarching theme is that **God's mercy precedes judgment**, giving opportunities for repentance before sending the promised scourge through Hazael.

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications about **pride and humility**. Ben-hadad's drunken arrogance illustrates how "security and sensuality went together" as forerunners of downfall. Ahab's wise proverb - "Let not him that girdeth on his harness boast himself as he that putteth it off" - becomes a spiritual principle: "While we are here we are but girding on the harness, and therefore must never boast as though we had put it off." 

The commentary emphasizes **the consequences of sin**, noting how "guilt dispirits men, and makes them cowards" and "a rebel to God is a slave to all besides." Henry warns about **the danger of compromise**, showing how Ahab's initial submission only emboldened his enemy's demands.

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides vivid character analysis throughout. On Ben-hadad's escalating demands (v. 5-6), he observes how the king was "as covetous as he is proud...as spiteful as he is haughty...as unreasonable as he is unjust." The commentary notes how enemies often target "whatsoever is pleasant in thine eyes" - what we most value becomes most vulnerable.

Regarding the drunken strategy session (v. 12, 16), Henry remarks: "How could he prosper that preferred his pleasure before his business?" This becomes a warning about leadership priorities and the folly of mixing indulgence with responsibility.

### Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this narrative to broader biblical themes, referencing **Hosea 2:8** about silver and gold prepared for Baal, **Luke 17:26** regarding security in the old world and Sodom, **Daniel 5** concerning Belshazzar's drunken downfall, and **Proverbs 27:1** about not boasting of tomorrow. He also references David's earlier subjugation of Syria and Asa's dangerous precedent of inviting foreign intervention.

The commentary effectively weaves this historical account into timeless spiritual principles about pride, divine sovereignty, and the consequences of moral compromise.
