# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Kings 13: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **divine sovereignty over judgment and mercy** throughout this narrative. The withering and restoration of Jeroboam's hand demonstrates that "He wounded, and no hand but his can make whole." The commentary stresses **God's patience with sinners**, noting the prophet was sent "in time, while he is but dedicating his altar, before his heart is hardened by the deceitfulness of his sin; for God delights not in the death of sinners."

A central theme is **the certainty of God's prophetic word**. Henry marvels that Josiah was named "above 300 years before he was born," demonstrating that "Nothing future is hidden from God." The remarkable 356-year prophecy shows divine foreknowledge transcends human contingency.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications about **obedience to divine commands**. The unnamed prophet's initial faithfulness shows "Those have little learned the lessons of self-denial that cannot forbear one forbidden meal." His later disobedience serves as a warning about compromise.

The commentary emphasizes **how believers should respond to persecution**: "Those only are entitled to the blessing Christ pronounced on the persecuted that learn of him to pray for their persecutors." The prophet's gracious intercession for Jeroboam models Christian virtue.

Henry warns against **fellowship with spiritual corruption**, noting God's command against eating in Beth-el was "to teach us not to have fellowship with the works of darkness, lest we have infection from them or give encouragement to them."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On **verse 2's prophecy**, Henry notes the message was directed "not to Jeroboam nor to the people, but to the altar, the stones of which would sooner hear and yield than those who were mad upon their idols."

Regarding **verse 4's judgment**, he observes: "When his hand was stretched out to burn incense to his calves it was not withered; but, when it is stretched out against a prophet, he shall have no use of it."

The **deceptive old prophet** (verses 11-22) represents how even God's servants can become instruments of temptation, highlighting the tragedy of compromised spiritual leaders.

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry connects this narrative to **Mark 16:20** regarding signs confirming God's messengers, and **Matthew 5:10, 44** on blessing persecutors. He references **Philippians 4:3** about "names in the book of the divine prescience" when discussing God's foreknowledge of Josiah.

The commentary links to **Exodus 10:17** comparing Jeroboam's superficial repentance to Pharaoh's, and **Ezekiel 2:8** regarding prophetic obedience.

Henry concludes with the sobering assessment: "Thy judgments, Lord, are a great deep," acknowledging the mysterious interplay of divine justice, human responsibility, and the tragic consequences of disobedience even among God's chosen messengers.
