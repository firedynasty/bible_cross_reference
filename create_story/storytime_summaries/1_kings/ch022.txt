# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Kings 22: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes the stark contrast between **true and false prophecy** as the central theological theme. He notes that "Unity is not always the mark of a true church and a true ministry" when describing the 400 false prophets who unanimously encouraged Ahab. The chapter demonstrates God's sovereignty in judgment—despite Ahab's temporary reprieve for his earlier humiliation, divine justice ultimately prevails.

The commentary highlights the **danger of unholy alliances**, particularly criticizing Jehoshaphat's "too great complaisance to evildoers" that brought him "into a dangerous fellowship with the unfruitful works of darkness." Henry sees this as a warning against compromising spiritual principles for political expedience.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Faithfulness in ministry** is paramount. He commends Micaiah's resolve: "What the Lord saith to me, that will I speak, without addition, diminution, or alteration." Ministers must prioritize God's truth over human approval.

**Discernment in spiritual matters** is essential. Henry notes that Jehoshaphat "could discern the fallacy" of the false prophets because he had "spiritual senses exercised." Believers must cultivate spiritual discernment to distinguish truth from deception.

**The danger of self-deception** appears in Ahab's hatred of Micaiah "because he doth not prophesy good concerning me, but evil." Henry observes: "Those are wretchedly hardened in sin...who hate God's ministers because they deal plainly with them."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides rich exposition throughout. On Ahab's three-year peace (v.1), he explains this as "a reward for his professions of repentance," though judgment remained certain. 

Regarding Jehoshaphat's unwise alliance (v.4), Henry notes the irony that "even bad men have often coveted the friendship of the good" while warning that good people can be ensnared through "unwariness."

On Micaiah's imprisonment, Henry observes that "his imprisonment had not excluded him from divine visits: the spirit of prophecy continued with him there. He was bound, but the word of the Lord was not."

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry connects this chapter to broader biblical themes, referencing Jeremiah 23:30 regarding false prophets who "stole the word from the true prophets." He links Jehoshaphat's alliance with Israel as atonement for "his father's fault in joining with Syria against Israel" (ch. 15:19-20).

The commentary emphasizes how this narrative illustrates timeless principles about divine justice, prophetic authenticity, and the consequences of compromising faith for worldly advantage.
