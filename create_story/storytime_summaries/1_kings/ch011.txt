# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Kings 11: Solomon's Fall

## Main Theological Themes

Henry opens with a "melancholy 'but'" that marks the tragic decline of Israel's greatest king. The central theological theme is the devastating consequence of apostasy, even for those specially blessed by God. Henry emphasizes that **"there is nothing perfect under the sun, but all is so above the sun,"** highlighting human frailty versus divine perfection.

The commentary stresses God's justice in judgment—since Solomon "revolted from God, part of his kingdom should revolt from his family." Yet divine mercy appears through God's covenant faithfulness to David, preserving one tribe for Solomon's lineage. Henry notes the **"most base ingratitude"** of Solomon's sin, given that God had appeared to him twice with explicit warnings.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several sobering warnings: **"Let him that thinks he stands take heed lest he fall."** He emphasizes our desperate need for God's grace and constant dependence upon it. 

The commentary warns about prosperity's dangers, noting Solomon "like Jeshurun, waxed fat and then kicked." Henry advocates for Agur's "food convenient" over Solomon's "food abundant." 

Particularly striking is Henry's observation about religious profession: those who make great shows of devotion face greater temptations because "the devil will set upon them most violently, and, if they misbehave, the reproach is the greater."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On Solomon's 1,000 wives and concubines, Henry notes **"not one good one among them"** and warns that **"the way of sin is down-hill; those that have got into it cannot easily stop themselves."** He suggests Solomon preferred foreign women because "daughters of Israel were too grave and modest for him."

Henry traces the progression: Solomon first grew "cool and indifferent" in worship, then tolerated his wives' idolatry, finally building pagan temples that confronted his own temple. The commentary emphasizes that it's **"easier to see a mischief, and to show it to others, than to shun it ourselves."**

## Cross-References and Scriptural Connections

Henry connects Solomon's fall to Nehemiah 13:26, Proverbs 7:26 (on women casting down many), and Deuteronomy 17:16-17 (prohibiting kings from multiplying horses and wives). He references David's similar failings and suggests Solomon presumed such behavior acceptable based on his father's example.

The commentary links to Israel's seduction by Moabite women at Baal-peor, showing recurring patterns of apostasy through foreign marriages. Henry concludes with the hope that **"it is the evening that commends the day,"** urging perseverance to the end.

This tragic account serves as scripture's faithful warning that even the wisest can fall through pride, sensual indulgence, and gradual compromise.
