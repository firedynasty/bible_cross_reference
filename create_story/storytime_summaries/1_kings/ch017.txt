# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Kings 17: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as a powerful demonstration of God's sovereignty and faithfulness amid national apostasy. The **central theological theme** is divine providence—how God cares for His servants even during judgment. Henry emphasizes that "never was Israel so blessed with a good prophet as when it was so plagued with a bad king," showing God's mercy alongside His justice.

The commentary highlights **God's absolute control over creation**, noting how the Lord "can make what use he pleases" of all creatures "either for judgment or mercy." Henry sees Elijah as a figure prefiguring John the Baptist, both sustained miraculously in wilderness settings.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several **practical lessons for believers**:

**Trust in Divine Providence**: "Let those who have but from hand to mouth learn to live upon Providence, and trust it for the bread of the day in the day." He encourages contentment with simple provision rather than envying those with "daintier fare."

**Submission to God's Timing**: When called to solitude, "it becomes us to acquiesce; when we cannot be useful we must be patient, and when we cannot work for God we must sit still quietly for him."

**Faith in Extremity**: Henry urges readers to "encourage ourselves in God in the greatest straits, and never to distrust him," pointing to God's ability to "furnish a table in the wilderness."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: Henry explains Elijah's name as "My God Jehovah is he" and notes how his obscure origins demonstrate that "we need not enquire whence men are, but what they are."

**Verses 4-6**: On the ravens feeding Elijah, Henry offers detailed observations: they were "birds of prey," "unclean creatures," yet became faithful providers. He notes the irony that "ravens neglect their own young ones...yet when God pleases they shall feed his prophet."

**Verse 7**: The brook's failure teaches that "the powers of nature are limited, but not the powers of the God of nature."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive biblical connections throughout:
- **James 5:17-18** on Elijah's prayer life
- **Revelation 11:6** linking to God's witnesses having power to shut heaven
- **Revelation 12:6,14** connecting the widow's three-and-a-half-year provision to prophetic symbolism
- **Jeremiah 14:22** on the impotence of heathen gods regarding rain
- **Psalm 34:10** and **Job 38:41** on God's providential care

The commentary presents Elijah as both **historical figure and prophetic type**, demonstrating God's faithfulness to sustain His servants during seasons of national judgment and personal testing.
