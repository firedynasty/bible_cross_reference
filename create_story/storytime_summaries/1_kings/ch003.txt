# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Kings 3: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes the transition from Solomon's "bloody" reign in chapter 2 to his merciful character in chapter 3, noting that "necessary acts of justice must not be called cruelty." The central theological theme is God's sovereignty in blessing those who prioritize divine worship and wisdom over personal gain.

The commentary highlights Solomon's love for God as foundational: "Solomon loved the LORD...the brightest encomium of him is that which is the character of all the saints, even the poorest, He loved the Lord." Henry connects this to the mystical relationship between God and His people, suggesting Solomon's marriage to Pharaoh's daughter may typify "the mystical espousals of the church to Christ, especially the Gentile church."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Prayer and Worship**: Solomon's generous offering of "a thousand burnt-offerings" demonstrates that "those that truly love God and his worship will not grudge the expenses of their religion. We may be tempted to say, To what purpose is this waste?...but we must never think that wasted which is laid out in the service of God."

**Humility in Leadership**: Solomon's prayer reveals proper leadership attitude: "Those who are employed in public stations ought to be very sensible of the weight and importance of their work and their own insufficiency for it." Henry notes that "the more knowing and considerate men are the better acquainted they are with their own weakness."

**Seeking Divine Wisdom**: Solomon requested "an understanding heart to judge thy people," showing that "the best knowledge...is which will be serviceable to us in doing our duty."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On Solomon's worship at high places (v. 2-3), Henry shows pastoral sensitivity: "while they worshipped God only...he graciously overlooked their weakness, and accepted their services...let not men be more severe than God is."

Regarding God's offer to Solomon (v. 5), Henry makes a powerful application: "God, in like manner, condescends to us...by assuring us that we shall have what we will for the asking...What would we more? Ask, and it shall be given you."

On Solomon's dream prayer, Henry observes that making "such an intelligent choice...when he was asleep...showed that it came purely from the grace of God, which wrought in him these gracious desires."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive biblical connections throughout:
- Links to 2 Samuel 12:24 regarding God's love for Solomon
- References Deuteronomy 12:5-6 on centralized worship
- Connects to John 16:23 and 1 John 5:14 on prayer promises
- Cites Psalm 116:16 on servant relationship with God
- References James 1:5 on requesting wisdom from God

The commentary demonstrates Henry's characteristic integration of Old Testament narrative with New Testament theology, making Solomon's experience relevant for Christian discipleship and leadership.
