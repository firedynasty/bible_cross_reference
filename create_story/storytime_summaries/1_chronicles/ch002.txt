# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Chronicles 2: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Grace and Sovereignty**: Henry emphasizes that God's covenant was "of grace, free grace" rather than works, noting that despite the poor character of Jacob's first four sons, "the covenant was entailed on their seed." He highlights God's sovereignty in family matters, stating that "Providence orders these affairs of families by an incontestable sovereignty, as pleaseth him."

**Messianic Focus**: The genealogy's primary purpose centers on Christ's lineage. Henry notes the tribe of Judah receives precedence "not so much for the sake of David as for the sake of the Son of David, our Lord, who sprang out of Judah" (Heb. 7:14). He calls this genealogy essential for "clearing the genealogy of Christ."

**Mixed Heritage**: Henry observes how God works through imperfect families, noting "The best and most honourable families may have those belonging to them that are blemishes," yet God's purposes prevail.

## Key Practical Applications

**Contentment in Childlessness**: For those without children, Henry offers pastoral comfort: "Let not those therefore that are written childless envy the families that are built up and replenished... To those that love him he will himself be better than ten sons, and give them in his house a place and a name better than of sons and daughters."

**Value of Learning**: Henry celebrates the mention of "families of scribes" who "kept up learning in their family, especially scripture-learning." He expresses the desire that "all the families of Israel [be] families of scribes, well instructed to the kingdom of heaven."

**Acceptance of Converts**: Regarding the Egyptian servant who married into an Israelite family, Henry teaches that "if Egyptians become converts, and servants do worthily, neither their parentage nor their servitude should be a bar to their preferment."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry highlights the contrast within Judah's lineage between the "very bad" (Er, Onan, Achan), the "very wise and good" (Heman, Ethan, Calcol, Dara), and the "very great" (Nahshon, Salmon). He notes David as "a seventh son" and observes that several genealogical lines terminate in place names rather than persons, indicating land inheritance patterns.

## Cross-References to Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout: Hebrews 7:14 (Christ from Judah), Isaiah 11:1 (rod from Jesse's stem), 1 Kings 4:31 (Solomon's wisdom compared to Zerah's descendants), Genesis 46:12 (Hezron among the seventy entering Egypt), Exodus 31:2 (Bezaleel as tabernacle craftsman), and Numbers 32:41 (Jair's achievements).

**Notable Quote**: "Would to God that all the Lord's people were prophets--all the families of Israel families of scribes, well instructed to the kingdom of heaven, and able to bring out of their treasury things new and old!"

Henry concludes that this detailed genealogy demonstrates "special providence" in preserving the records necessary to authenticate Christ's royal and tribal lineage through Judah.
