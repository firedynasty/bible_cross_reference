# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Chronicles 10: The Death of Saul

## Main Theological Themes

Matthew Henry emphasizes divine justice as the central theme, showing how "the sin of sinners will certainly find them out, sooner or later." He presents Saul's death as God's righteous judgment, noting the ironic parallels: Saul threw javelins at David but was himself wounded by archers; he commanded others to kill but ended up killing himself. Henry observes that "What is done by wicked hands is yet done by the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God."

The commentary also explores the consequences of leadership sin, demonstrating how "Princes sin and the people suffer for it." Though Israel had been reformed under Samuel and wasn't currently practicing idolatry, they suffered because of Saul's transgressions.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several sobering lessons about the far-reaching effects of sin:

**For Leaders**: "Princes and great men should in a special manner take heed of provoking God's wrath; for, if they kindle that fire, they know not how many may be consumed by it for their sakes."

**For Parents**: "Those that love their seed must leave their sins, lest they perish not alone in their iniquity, but bring ruin on their families with themselves, or entail a curse upon them when they are gone."

**For All**: Henry warns that "no man's greatness can exempt him from the judgments of God" and that "disobedience is a killing thing."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry emphasizes that Ezra records Saul's death (not his reign) to show how "way was made for David to the throne," connecting to the ultimate Son of David. He notes the tragic irony that Jonathan, "that gracious, generous man," perished with his father, showing that "all things come alike to all."

Regarding the Philistines' treatment of Saul's body, Henry observes their dedication to their false gods: "Shall Dagon have so honourable a share in their triumphs and the true and living God be forgotten in ours?"

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry frequently references 1 Samuel 31 for the parallel account and 1 Samuel 26:10 where David foresaw Saul's eventual downfall. He connects Saul's consultation with the witch to 1 Samuel 28:6, noting the distinction between Saul's late, desperate inquiry of God versus his failure to consistently seek the Lord.

The commentary concludes with the theological tension that while "Saul slew himself," ultimately "God slew him," demonstrating divine sovereignty over human actions and the principle that "Those that abandon themselves to the devil shall be abandoned to him."
