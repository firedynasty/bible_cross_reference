# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Chronicles 17 - Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as fundamentally about God's gracious covenant promises and their Messianic fulfillment. While David cannot build the temple, God promises to "build him a house" - establishing his dynasty. Most significantly, Henry interprets verses 11-14 as pointing beyond Solomon to Christ, "who should descend from his loins, and whose throne should be established for evermore." The Messiah will have "all power both in heaven and in earth, in the house and in the kingdom" as both priest and king.

Henry emphasizes God's sovereignty in directing His people's service, noting that "God graciously accepts his people's good purposes, yea, though he himself prevents the performance of them." This demonstrates that God values willing hearts over completed works.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**On Service**: "What pleasure can we take in our own prosperity if we see not the good of Jerusalem?" David's concern for the ark while dwelling in luxury exemplifies how "the concerns of the public should always be near our hearts."

**On Contentment**: Believers should accept humble circumstances, as "God's ark was content with a tabernacle" and "we may well be content awhile with mean accommodations."

**On Unfulfilled Purposes**: When God prevents our good intentions, "it being God's act to prevent the execution of it, he shall be as fully recompensed as if he had done it." The reward matches the willing heart.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry highlights key differences between Chronicles and 2 Samuel. In verse 17, where Samuel asks "Is this the manner of men?" Chronicles declares God "hast regarded me according to the estate of a man of high degree." Henry notes this shows how God "regards them according to the estate of men of high degree, though they are mean and vile."

Verse 19's phrase "for thy servant's sake" (versus Samuel's "for thy word's sake") points to Christ, since "Jesus Christ is both the Word of God and the servant of God, and it is for his sake...that the promises are both made and made good to all believers."

## Cross-References and Scriptural Connections

Henry extensively connects this passage to Christ's dual nature and eternal kingdom, referencing Zechariah 6:12-13 about the priest-king who "shall build the temple of the Lord." He links David's prayer to Hebrews 11:13, showing how "by faith he receives the promises, embraces them, and is persuaded of them, as the patriarchs."

The commentary concludes with David's transformed perspective from petition to faith: while 2 Samuel ends with "let the house of thy servant be blessed," Chronicles declares with certainty: "For thou blessest, O Lord! and it shall be blessed for ever." Henry notes this progression "from the language of a holy desire" to "the language of a most holy faith," demonstrating that "those whom God blesses are truly and eternally blessed."
