# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Chronicles Chapter 7 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry views this genealogical chapter as a testament to God's faithfulness despite apparent setbacks. He emphasizes divine providence in both judgment and mercy, particularly noting that "God's providences often seem to contradict his promises; but, when they do so, they really magnify the promise, and make the performance of it, notwithstanding, so much more illustrious."

The omission of Dan from the genealogies receives theological interpretation: Henry attributes this to Dan's association with idolatry, stating "it is the disgrace of the tribe of Dan that idolatry began in that colony of the Danites which fixed in Laish." This demonstrates how unfaithfulness can lead to divine disapproval and removal from covenant privileges.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from the tribal accounts:

**Industry and Preparation**: Regarding Issachar, he notes they were "inured to labour and business" and concludes that "Those that were...the fittest to serve their country when there was occasion." He advocates against idleness: "Let no people complain of their numbers, provided they suffer none to be idle."

**Learning from Family Mistakes**: Commenting on Manasseh's intermarriage issues, Henry observes: "It is good for the children to take warning by their father's mistakes and not stumble at the same stone."

**Divine Timing**: From Ephraim's premature military action, he warns: "Those that will not wait God's time cannot expect God's blessing."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides detailed analysis of Ephraim's tragic losses (verses 20-27), interpreting this as either raids by Philistine giants from Gath or premature Israelite aggression. He emphasizes Ephraim's extended mourning and the comfort provided by his brothers, noting the significance of the name "Beriah" (meaning "in trouble") as reflecting life's inherent difficulties.

He highlights Sherah's city-building achievements, concluding that "A virtuous woman may be as great an honour and blessing to a family as a mighty man," demonstrating progressive views on women's contributions.

Regarding Asher's smaller but elite military force, Henry suggests wisdom in quality over quantity: "perhaps it was their wisdom that they coveted not to make their trained bands numerous, but rather to have a few, and those apt to the war."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects these genealogies to broader biblical narrative:
- Links Issachar to Jacob's blessing in Genesis 49:14
- References Benjamin's prophetic description as a wolf (Genesis 49:27)
- Connects to the account of Joshua's census in 2 Samuel 24
- Draws parallels between Beriah and Seth as replacement children (Genesis 4:25)
- References the ultimate fulfillment of promises despite setbacks (2 Samuel 23:5)
- Notes the pattern of divine comfort in Psalms 90:13-15

Henry's commentary transforms these genealogical lists into lessons about faithfulness, divine providence, practical wisdom, and the importance of maintaining covenant relationship with God across generations.
