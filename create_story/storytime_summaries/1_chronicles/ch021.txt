# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Chronicles 21: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **pride as the root sin** behind David's census, noting "there is no sin that has in it more of contradiction and therefore more of offence to God than pride." He explores the theological tension between divine sovereignty and Satan's agency, explaining that while "Satan stood up against Israel and provoked David," God permitted this for "wise and holy ends" since "God is not the author of sin—he tempts no man."

The commentary highlights **God's merciful judgment**, showing how divine correction serves to drive out "the foolishness that is bound up in the heart." Henry observes that even God's "dearest children" must expect discipline when they sin, yet God's mercy remains paramount.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons: **Spiritual vigilance is essential** since "even the best saints, till they come to heaven, must never think themselves out of the reach of Satan's temptations." He warns that "the devil does us more mischief by tempting us to sin against our God than he does by accusing us before our God."

**Proper stewardship** emerges in David's insistence to purchase Ornan's threshing floor, declaring "I will not take that which is thine for the LORD, nor offer burnt offerings without cost." Henry emphasizes that true worship requires personal sacrifice, not convenience.

**Humble submission** under God's correction is modeled by David, who "cast himself upon the mercy of God" and demonstrated "tender concern for the people."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Regarding David's choice of punishment, Henry notes David's wisdom in choosing pestilence: "Let us fall into the hands of the Lord, for his mercies are great." This shows that "good men, even when God frowns upon them, think well of him."

Henry observes the irony in verse 14: "David must have the people numbered...But now God numbers them after another manner, numbers to the sword." What David counted in pride, God reduced in judgment.

The vision of the destroying angel demonstrates how "while we lie under the wrath of God the holy angels are armed against us, though we see them not as David did."

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry connects this account with **2 Samuel 24** and references **Job 2:3** regarding Satan's accusations. He links David's experience to **Luke 22:26** about leadership humility and **Isaiah 65:12** about divine numbering for judgment.

The commentary significantly connects this event to **the future temple site**, noting that "in the atonement made for that sin, an intimation was given of the spot of ground on which the temple should be built." This transforms judgment into redemptive purpose.

Henry concludes with the powerful image of God's mercy: "When David repented of the sin God repented of the judgment," showing how genuine repentance turns away divine wrath and establishes lasting peace.
