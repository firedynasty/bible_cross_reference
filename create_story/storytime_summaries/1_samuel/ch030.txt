# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 30 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as a demonstration of divine providence and redemption through trial. He emphasizes how "the righteous is delivered out of trouble and the wicked cometh in his stead," showing God's pattern of bringing His people low before raising them up. The Amalekite raid serves as divine correction for David's compromise with the Philistines, illustrating how "when we go abroad in the way of our duty we may comfortably hope that God will take care of our families in our absence, but not otherwise."

Henry notes the providential restraint shown when the Amalekites spared the captives rather than killing them, attributing this mercy to God's sovereign control: "he has all hearts in his hands, and says to the fury of the most cruel men, Hitherto thou shalt come, and no further."

## Key Practical Applications

The commentary emphasizes the importance of seeking God's guidance before taking action. David's consultation of the ephod demonstrates proper dependence on divine direction, even in seemingly obvious situations. Henry warns against the compromise that led to David's troubles, noting that "those that make bold with others must expect that others will make as bold with them."

Most significantly, Henry highlights David's response to crisis: "David encouraged himself in the Lord his God." This becomes the model for believers facing overwhelming circumstances - turning to God rather than giving way to despair or rage like David's men who "spoke of stoning him."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry observes that David's greatest trial came "just before his accession to the throne," suggesting that "things are sometimes at the worst with the church and people of God just before they begin to mend." The commentary notes how David's patient response to his men's threats - treating them with "gentleness" rather than retaliation - demonstrates that "meekness is the security of any government."

Regarding the providential discovery of the Egyptian servant, Henry sees God's hand in providing crucial intelligence. The complete recovery of all captives and spoil illustrates God's faithfulness to His promises.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry references several biblical parallels: Romans 8:28 regarding God working all things for good, Proverbs 27:1 about not boasting of tomorrow, Numbers 27:21 concerning proper use of the ephod for guidance, and 1 Chronicles 15:13 about learning from past negligence in seeking the Lord.

The commentary connects this event to the broader pattern of God's dealings with His people, showing how afflictions serve to "remind by them of neglected duties, and particularly to be quickened by them to enquire of the Lord." Henry presents this chapter as a template for faithful response to crisis through dependence on God rather than human wisdom or emotion.
