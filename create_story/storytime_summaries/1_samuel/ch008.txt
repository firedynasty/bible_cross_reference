# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 8: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry identifies this chapter as a tragic turning point where Israel rejects divine governance for worldly monarchy. **The central theological theme is rejection of God's rule**: "They have not rejected thee, but they have rejected me, that I should not reign over them" (v. 7). Henry emphasizes that Israel possessed unique divine favor through prophetic leadership, being "great and happy above any nation, none having God so nigh unto them" (referencing Deuteronomy 4:7), yet they "foolishly forsake their own mercies" by demanding external pomp over spiritual substance.

The commentary presents this as part of Israel's pattern of apostasy: "According to all the works which they have done since the day that I brought them up out of Egypt...so do they also unto thee" (v. 8). Henry sees their desire to be "like all the nations" as fundamentally abandoning their distinctive calling as God's chosen people.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several sobering lessons about leadership succession and human nature:

**On aging leadership**: Samuel's displacement despite faithful service illustrates ingratitude toward those who have served well. Henry notes it was "very unkind, ungrateful, nay, and unjust, to cast him off when he was old, who had spent his days in doing them good."

**On generational failure**: Even godly leaders cannot guarantee their children's faithfulness. Henry observes that "those that have the most grace themselves cannot give grace to their children," and power often corrupts: "Many that have done well in a state of meanness and subjection have been spoiled by preferment and power."

**On discernment**: The people prioritized appearance over substance, wanting "any body that will but make a figure" rather than wise, godly leadership.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides rich observations throughout:

- **Verses 1-3**: Samuel's premature aging resulted from early responsibility: "he was a man betimes...which perhaps hastened the infirmities of age upon him. The fruits that are the first ripe keep the worst."

- **Verse 3**: On judicial corruption, Henry notes the sons "turned aside after the mammon of unrighteousness" and emphasizes that "the love of money is the root of all evil...especially in judges."

- **Verse 6**: Samuel's response models proper leadership: rather than react defensively to personal criticism, he was troubled only when God's honor was at stake, then "prayed unto the Lord for direction."

- **Verses 11-18**: The detailed warning about kingship shows how monarchy will burden them with taxation, conscription, and service, culminating in the sobering prediction: "ye shall cry out in that day...and the LORD will not hear you."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout:
- **Deuteronomy 4:7** - Israel's unique proximity to God
- **Judges 9:22** - Abimelech's failed kingship as historical precedent  
- **Philippians 4:6** - Encouragement to bring troubles to God in prayer
- **Psalm 69:7** and **Colossians 1:24** - Bearing reproach for God's sake
- **Job's description** of children "marring their path"
- **Chapter 12:3** - Samuel's integrity contrasted with his sons' corruption

Henry concludes with the memorable observation: "Thus hard is it for people to know when they are well off," capturing the human tendency to abandon proven blessings for untested alternatives.
