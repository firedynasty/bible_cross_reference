# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 19: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents the chapter as a demonstration of God's providential care, noting that **"God has many ways of preserving his people. Providence is never at a loss."** The commentary explores the spiritual warfare between good and evil, emphasizing how Saul's jealousy represents the "enmity of the serpent against that of the woman." 

Henry highlights the danger of harboring sinful passions, observing that **"when we let the sun go down upon our wrath we give place to the devil."** He connects Saul's evil spirit to his own choices, noting that "discomposures of mind, though helped forward by the agency of Satan, commonly owe their origin to men's own sins and follies."

The commentary also addresses the corrupting nature of unchecked malice, describing how **"so deceitful and desperately wicked is the heart of man without the grace of God"** (referencing Jeremiah 17:9).

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

1. **Perseverance in well-doing despite mistreatment**: David continued serving faithfully even when "ill paid for doing good," demonstrating that we "must not be weary of well doing, remembering what a bountiful benefactor our heavenly Father is, even to the froward and unthankful."

2. **The value of faithful friendship**: Jonathan exemplifies true friendship by "speaking good of him, though he was in danger of incurring his father's displeasure"—described as "a rare instance of valuable friendship!"

3. **Humble service**: David's willingness to continue playing his harp shows that "a humble man will think nothing below him by which he may do good."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides detailed analysis of Jonathan's intercession (verses 4-5), praising its wisdom and noting how Jonathan appealed to David's **"good services"** and **"innocency."** He observes that Jonathan's argument was both prudent and "prevalent" because "God inclined the heart of Saul to hearken."

Regarding Saul's javelin attack (verses 9-10), Henry notes the irony that Saul's fresh military success against the Philistines, rather than confirming reconciliation, "revived his envy and exasperated him yet more."

For Michal's deception (verses 11-17), Henry comments that she "proved to be his protector and helper" rather than the snare Saul intended, observing that **"often is the devil out-shot with his own bow."**

## Cross-References to Scripture

Henry references several biblical connections:
- **Psalm 59** as David's composition during this crisis
- **Psalm 35:13-14** regarding David's attitude toward his sick enemy
- **Ephesians 4:26-27** about not giving place to the devil through sustained anger
- **Jeremiah 17:9** concerning the human heart's deceptiveness

The commentary consistently connects David's experiences to his later psalmic expressions about God being his "shield and buckler, his rock and fortress."
