# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Samuel 9: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes God's sovereignty in establishing Israel's monarchy through divine providence rather than human ambition. He notes the remarkable fact that "a crown, such a crown, set up, and nobody bids for it," highlighting how Israel's government began uniquely "in the ambition of the people to be ruled" rather than princes seeking to rule. 

The commentary contrasts God's choice of kings: when choosing "a king after the people's heart," God selected Saul for his impressive physical stature, but when choosing "a king after his own heart" (David), God looked beyond outward appearance to character. Henry sees this as divine accommodation to Israel's worldly desires for a king "like the nations."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several moral lessons from Saul's humble beginnings seeking lost donkeys:

- **Faithful service in small matters**: "Seest thou a man diligent in his business, and dutiful to his superiors...he does as Saul stand fair for preferment"
- **Filial obedience**: Saul's concern for his father's worry demonstrates how "children should take care that they do nothing to grieve or frighten their parents"
- **Divine providence in ordinary events**: Saul "missed of what he sought, but...met with the kingdom, which he never dreamed of"

Henry criticizes the shallow spirituality of consulting God's prophet merely about lost livestock rather than seeking spiritual guidance: "Most people would rather be told their fortune than told their duty."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry notes Saul came from Benjamin, "the smallest of the tribes," showing God gives "more abundant honour to that part which lacked." His physical description emphasizes appearance over character—"No mention is here made of his wisdom or virtue."

**Verses 6-8**: The servant's respectful words about Samuel—"He is a man of God, and an honourable man"—prompt Henry's observation that "Men of God are honourable men" and "all he saith comes surely to pass" was Samuel's consistent reputation.

**Verse 9**: Henry explains the historical note about prophets being called "Seers" as reflecting their role as "seeing men."

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry connects this passage to several scriptures:
- **Deuteronomy 17:15** regarding God's right to choose Israel's king
- **Hannah's song (1 Samuel 2:8)** about God raising "the poor out of the dust"
- **Romans 11:1 and Philippians 3:5** noting Benjamin as Paul's honored tribe
- **1 Samuel 16:7,12** contrasting David's selection based on heart rather than appearance
- **1 Corinthians 12:24** about God honoring what seems lacking

Henry concludes with measured optimism about these "hopeful and promising" beginnings, while noting the tragic irony "that the sin of the people was the spring of this great affair."
