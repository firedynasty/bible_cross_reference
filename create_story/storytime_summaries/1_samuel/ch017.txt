# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Samuel 17: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as divine Providence positioning David for kingship, marking him as "a man after God's own heart." The narrative contrasts human strength versus divine power, with Goliath representing worldly might and David embodying faith-dependent courage. 

A central theme is God's sovereignty in timing - Jonathan, the previous champion, "must now sit still, because the honour of engaging Goliath is reserved for David." Henry emphasizes that "the best, so the bravest men, are no more than what God makes them," highlighting divine empowerment over human ability.

The commentary also addresses Israel's spiritual condition, noting that "Israel's ground would never have been footing for Philistine-armies if Israel had been faithful to their God." The enemies' advantage came when "her protectors have provoked God's Spirit and prophets to leave them," referring to Samuel's departure from Saul.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several moral lessons from David's character:

**Humility and Family Devotion**: David's return to shepherding after court service demonstrates "a rare instance, in a young man that stood so fair for preferment, of humility and affection to his parents." He was "none more dead to" honor despite deserving it most.

**Business as Medicine**: Henry notes that "Business is a good antidote against melancholy" when discussing Saul's temporary relief from his affliction during wartime preparations.

**Divine Timing in Service**: The commentary emphasizes that "In great and good actions, the wind of the Spirit blows when and where he listeth," teaching patience in waiting for God's calling.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-11 (Goliath's Challenge)**: Henry provides detailed calculations of Goliath's height (eleven feet, four inches) and describes him as having a heart "nothing but a lump of proud flesh." The giant's challenge is seen as mere "bravado" since nations wouldn't truly stake everything on single combat.

**Verses 12-30 (David's Arrival)**: Henry emphasizes the providence in David's mundane errand to bring food to his brothers, noting how God uses ordinary circumstances for extraordinary purposes. David's question "who is this uncircumcised Philistine, that he should defy the armies of the living God?" reveals his theological perspective versus the army's focus on material rewards.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this narrative to several biblical themes:

- References **Joshua 11:22** regarding the giants of Gath who remained from Joshua's conquest
- Links to **1 Samuel 11:6** contrasting Saul's earlier Spirit-empowered courage against Nahash with his current fearfulness
- Connects to **1 Samuel 16:23** regarding David's previous service with his harp

The commentary positions this chapter within salvation history, showing how God prepares David through both court service (as "Saul's physician") and military victory (as "Israel's champion") for his destined throne.

Henry's interpretation emphasizes that this familiar story demonstrates God's pattern of using the humble and faithful to accomplish His purposes, while showing how spiritual decline leads to military vulnerability and how divine timing orchestrates human events for His glory.
