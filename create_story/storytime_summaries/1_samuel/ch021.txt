# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 21 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents this chapter as a study in human weakness under pressure, emphasizing that David's struggles serve as both warning and comfort to believers. He notes that David functions as "a type of Christ, who, being anointed to the kingdom, humbled himself," yet unlike Jesus, David's example "was not a copy without a blot." 

The central theological lesson centers on how "troubles are justly called temptations, for many are by them drawn into sin." Henry uses David's moral failures to demonstrate the "weakness and infirmity of good men" and warns that "the best are not perfect on this side heaven." The commentary emphasizes God's mercy in recording these failures not for imitation but "for our admonition."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from David's deception and flight:

**On David's lies to Ahimelech:** Henry condemns this "gross untruth" as unnecessary, noting it "proved of bad consequence" and led to the priests' deaths. He emphasizes that "Had he trusted God aright, he would not have used such a sorry sinful shift" and warns: "Let him that thinks he stands take heed lest he fall."

**On seeking God in trouble:** Despite David's failures, Henry commends his turning to the tabernacle, noting "it is a great comfort to us in a day of trouble that we have a God to go to." He sees David's consultation with the priest as proper seeking of divine direction.

**On faithfulness in adversity:** Henry observes how Ahimelech "looked shy upon him, as most are apt to do upon their friends when the world frowns upon them," highlighting the fickleness of human support.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2:** Henry notes David's emotional farewell to the tabernacle, knowing "nothing will be more afflictive to him in his banishment than his distance from the house of God." He observes the dramatic reversal: "He that was suddenly advanced from the solitude of a shepherd's life to the crowd and hurries of the camp is now as soon reduced to the desolate condition of an exile."

**Verses 4-6:** On the showbread incident, Henry argues that David correctly understood that "ritual observance must give way to moral duties" in cases of "urgent providential necessity." He notes Christ's later approval of this reasoning in Matthew 12:3-4.

**Verses 8-9:** Regarding Goliath's sword, Henry sees divine providence: "God had graciously given it to him, as a pledge of his singular favour" and notes that David had "gratefully given it back to God," demonstrating that "what we devote to God's praise...is most likely to redound...to our own comfort and benefit."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry makes several significant biblical connections:

- **Mark 2:26** - Links to Christ's defense of the showbread incident
- **Matthew 12:3-4** - Jesus using this event to teach that "mercy is to be preferred to sacrifice"
- **Psalm 42:4** - David's longing for corporate worship
- **Psalm 37:25** - On the righteous occasionally lacking bread
- **1 Thessalonians 4:4-5** - On believers' bodies being "possessed in sanctification"
- **Malachi 1:13** - Describing Doeg's attitude toward worship
- **Galatians 2:4** - Warning about false brethren in sacred places

The commentary consistently portrays this chapter as prefiguring Christ while warning against presuming on grace, emphasizing that even great saints can fall when faith weakens under pressure.
