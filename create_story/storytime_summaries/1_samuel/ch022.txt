# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Samuel 22: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as a study in contrasts between divine providence and human wickedness. **David's exile represents God's mysterious ways**: "We must not think it strange if sometimes shining lights be thus eclipsed and hidden under a bushel." Henry sees David's suffering as prefiguring Christ, noting that "The Son of David is ready to receive distressed souls, that will appoint him their captain and be commanded by him."

The commentary emphasizes **God's sovereignty in using unlikely instruments**—David's ragtag army of "men in distress, in debt, and discontented" demonstrates how "weak instruments God sometimes makes use of, by which to bring about his own purposes." Meanwhile, Saul's progression into tyranny shows how "jealous malice" corrupts leadership and judgment.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Family obligations remain paramount**: David's care for his aged parents teaches that "children learn from this to show pity at home and requite their parents," regardless of circumstances or status.

**Humble faith in trials**: David's statement "Till I know what God will do for me" exemplifies proper Christian attitude—"expecting a good issue, not from his own arts, or arms, or merits, but from what the wisdom, power, and goodness of God would do for him."

**Seeking godly counsel**: Even though David was "himself a prophet," he welcomed Gad's guidance, showing that spiritual leaders should remain teachable.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry connects **verse 1** to Psalm 142 and Hebrews 11:38, showing David among the Old Testament worthies who "wandered in deserts, in dens and caves of the earth."

Regarding **verses 2-4**, Henry notes the irony that David's first followers were society's outcasts, yet his tender care for his parents showed his character remained intact.

For **verses 6-8**, Henry analyzes Saul's paranoid reasoning, observing how "if a ruler hearken to lies, all his servants are wicked"—at least in his perception.

The massacre at Nob (**verses 17-19**) highlights how even Saul's servants initially "would not put forth their hand to fall upon the priests of the LORD," making Doeg's compliance especially heinous.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves multiple scriptural connections throughout: Hebrews 11:38 (David among faithful sufferers), 1 Timothy 5:4 (honoring parents), Psalm 27:10 (when parents forsake, God receives), Proverbs 29:12 (rulers and lies), and 1 Chronicles 11:15 (David's mighty men). He also references Psalm 142 as David's composition during his cave experience.

The commentary consistently types David's experiences as foreshadowing Christ's ministry to outcasts and His suffering at the hands of religious and political authorities.
