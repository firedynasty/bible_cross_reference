# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 16: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes God's sovereignty in choosing David over human expectations. The central theological principle appears in verse 7: **"The LORD seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart."** Henry contrasts human judgment based on physical appearance with God's evaluation of inner character and spiritual disposition.

The commentary highlights God's providence in raising "those of low degree" while passing by seemingly more qualified candidates. Henry notes that "God does not" follow human patterns of inheritance based on "seniority of age and priority of birth," demonstrating divine sovereignty over human customs.

Henry presents David as "an illustrious a type of Christ," drawing parallels between David's humble origins and Jesus as "he whom men despised, the stone which the builders refused." The symbolism extends to David's anointing with a horn of oil (versus Saul's glass vial), representing something "more plentiful and durable."

## Key Practical Applications

**On Excessive Grief**: Henry addresses Samuel's prolonged mourning for Saul, teaching that while grief is natural, we must "acquiesce in the divine justice" and prioritize "public welfare before private affection." God asks Samuel: "How long wilt thou mourn for Saul?"

**On Judging Others**: The commentary warns against superficial judgments, encouraging readers to value "true beauty which is within" and judge people "by their minds, not their mien." 

**On Spiritual Preparation**: Before participating in worship, Henry emphasizes the need for sanctification: "Before solemn ordinances there must be a solemn protestation" and "those that come to sacrifice should come peaceably."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-5**: Henry explains Samuel's fear of Saul as evidence of the king's growing wickedness, while noting that even "the best men are not perfect in their faith." God's instruction to cover the anointing with a sacrifice demonstrates divine wisdom in protecting His servants.

**Verses 6-10**: Henry observes Samuel's ironic repetition of choosing by appearance despite his experience with Saul, showing how even prophets can err when acting without direct divine guidance.

**Verses 11-13**: David's absence from the family gathering while "keeping the sheep" illustrates how "many a great genius lies buried in obscurity." Henry sees pastoral life as ideal preparation for leadership, providing "advantage for contemplation and communion with heaven."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects David's story to broader biblical themes through multiple references: Luke 1:69 ("horn of salvation"), Acts 13:22 (man after God's heart), Psalm 89:20, Matthew 2:3 (Jerusalem troubled at Christ's birth), and 1 Peter 3:4 (inner beauty). He parallels David's humble origins with Moses being called "from keeping the flock of Jethro" and references Job 1:5 regarding family sacrifices.

The commentary consistently points toward Christ, noting that **"when our Lord Jesus came into the world...he came peaceably, for he came to sacrifice."**
