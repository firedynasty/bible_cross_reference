## Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 11 Summary

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry emphasizes **God's sovereign providence** throughout this chapter, noting that while Israel wrongly demanded a king, "God did not reject them when they rejected him." He demonstrates how God works through circumstances—even using the enemy's overconfidence for Israel's deliverance. 

The commentary highlights **divine enablement for leadership**, showing how "the Spirit of God came upon Saul" when crisis arose. Henry notes God's wisdom in choosing someone who, "if he did not find fit, yet he made fit" for the great trust of kingship.

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**Humility in leadership**: Despite being anointed king, Saul continued tending his flocks, showing he "was not puffed up with his advancement." Like Paul, he worked with his hands rather than burden the people.

**The role of godly fear in citizenship**: When Israel responded to Saul's call, "the fear of the LORD fell on the people." Henry notes, "Religion and the fear of God will make men good subjects, good soldiers, and good friends to the public interests."

**Proper response to crisis**: Good magistrates should be "in pain if their subjects are in tears," as demonstrated by Saul's immediate concern when finding his people weeping.

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3**: Henry explains the Ammonites' cruel demand to remove right eyes would disable soldiers (who fought with shields covering their left eye) and "put a reproach upon all Israel" for their inability to rescue their brethren.

**Verse 6**: The divine enablement is crucial—"the Spirit of God came upon Saul...and his anger was kindled greatly." This was "a brave and generous fire" appropriate for his high calling.

**Verse 7**: Henry praises Saul's modesty in "joining Samuel in commission with himself" and his measured approach—threatening only the oxen, not the owners, showing "his government was more gentle."

### Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this narrative to broader biblical themes through multiple references:

- **Deuteronomy 2:19** - regarding Israel's relationship with Ammon
- **Judges 21:10** - the earlier destruction of Jabesh-Gilead  
- **Proverbs 27:23-24** - wisdom of maintaining practical affairs since "the crown doth not endure to every generation"
- **Job 25:3** - comparing earthly armies to God's innumerable hosts
- **Deuteronomy 28:31** - the threatened judgment of losing livestock

The commentary presents this chapter as demonstrating God's faithfulness despite Israel's unfaithfulness, while establishing principles for godly leadership that balance divine calling with practical wisdom and humble service.
