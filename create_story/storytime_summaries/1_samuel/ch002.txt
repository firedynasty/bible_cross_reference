# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Samuel 2: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Hannah's song as both thanksgiving and prophecy, emphasizing **God's sovereignty over all earthly circumstances**. The central theme is divine reversal - God's power to dramatically change human fortunes according to His will. Henry highlights four key divine attributes Hannah celebrates: God's "unspotted purity" (holiness), "almighty power," "unsearchable wisdom," and "unerring justice."

The commentary emphasizes that **God alone deserves praise as "a peerless being, and of unparalleled perfection."** Henry notes the prophetic nature of Hannah's prayer, connecting it to the Virgin Mary's Magnificat and seeing it as encouragement for "those of the weaker sex to attend the throne of grace."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

- **Gratitude for answered prayer**: "What we win by prayer we may wear with comfort, and must wear with praise"
- **Contentment in circumstances**: Whether rich or poor, "it is God's doing" - the poor should "be content," while the rich should "be thankful and serve him cheerfully"
- **Humility regarding prosperity**: "Let not the rich be proud and secure, for God can soon make them poor"
- **Hope in adversity**: "Let not the poor despond and despair, for God can in due time enrich them again"

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry observes that Hannah "overlooks the gift, and praises the giver; whereas most forget the giver and fasten only on the gift." Her declaration "My horn is exalted" means her "praises are very much elevated to an unusual strain."

Regarding divine reversals (verses 4-7), Henry notes these demonstrate that "it is often found a very short step between the height of prosperity and the depth of adversity." He explains "the barren hath borne seven" as Hannah speaking prophetically of her eventual five additional children, making Samuel count "as good as seven" due to his dedication to God.

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry extensively connects this passage to other scriptures, notably linking Hannah's song to **Luke 1:46 (Mary's Magnificat)**, emphasizing their "great affinity." He references Psalm 18:31 regarding God's uniqueness, connects to Moses' song in Exodus 15:11 about God being "glorious in holiness," and cites multiple Psalms regarding divine justice and sovereignty.

The commentary presents this chapter as a microcosm of God's dealings with humanity - demonstrating through personal experience the greater theological truth that "the Lord shall judge the ends of the earth."
