# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 6: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes God's sovereignty and justice through the ark's seven-month captivity among the Philistines. He notes that "wherever they carried the ark, so long as they carried it captive, they should find it a curse to them," demonstrating that God cannot be controlled or contained by human power. The Philistines' plagues serve as divine judgment, while their eventual submission reveals God's authority over all nations, not just Israel.

A key theme is the contrast between true and false worship. Henry criticizes the Philistines' "wretchedly vain imaginations" in offering golden images as trespass offerings, noting that "it is blood, and not gold, that makes atonement for the soul" - pointing toward proper biblical sacrifice.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several moral lessons: First, "Sinners lengthen out their own miseries by obstinately refusing to part with their sins," comparing the Philistines to Pharaoh's hardened heart. He advocates learning from others' experiences rather than repeating their mistakes: "It is much cheaper to learn by other people's experience than by our own."

For believers separated from public worship, Henry offers comfort: "we may have God nigh unto us when the ark is at a distance" through "faith and prayer." He encourages trusting God's presence even when outward symbols are absent.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry highlights the miraculous nature of the cows' behavior (verses 10-12), noting how they went "contrary to their natural instinct and inclination" directly to Beth-shemesh, "lowing as they went" - showing both their divine compulsion and natural reluctance to leave their calves.

The golden offerings (verses 4-5) represent the Philistines "take shame to themselves as conquered and yielding" while giving "glory to the God of Israel as their mighty conqueror." Their willingness to use gold showed they "would gladly purchase their peace with the God of Israel at any rate."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects the Philistines' experience to Egypt's plagues, noting how both Pharaoh and the Philistine priests reference this precedent (verse 6). He cites Romans 1:18 regarding those who "imprisoned the truth in unrighteousness" and references Deuteronomy 28:16 about being "cursed in the city, and cursed in the field."

He also draws parallels to Malachi 3:10 and Haggai 2:18-19 regarding testing God through reformation, and connects the ark's return to Psalm 105:38 about Egypt being "glad when they departed."

Henry masterfully weaves together themes of divine sovereignty, proper worship, and God's universal authority while drawing practical lessons about obstinate sin and trusting God's presence in difficult circumstances.
