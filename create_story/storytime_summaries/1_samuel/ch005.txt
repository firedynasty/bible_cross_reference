# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 5: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Sovereignty Over False Gods**: Henry emphasizes God's supremacy by showing how the ark triumphs over Dagon despite appearing defeated. He notes that "God would take the work into his own hands and plead his own cause, since men would not appear for him." The repeated falling of Dagon demonstrates that "the kingdom of Satan will certainly fall before the kingdom of Christ."

**God's Self-Vindication**: A central theme is God defending His own honor when His people fail to do so. Henry observes that God "is bringing about his own glory even when he seems to neglect it," showing divine patience followed by decisive action.

**Judgment and Mercy**: The commentary reveals God's justice in punishing both Israel's unfaithfulness and Philistine arrogance, while simultaneously working toward the ark's restoration.

## Key Practical Applications

**Spiritual Apathy Warning**: Henry criticizes Israel's passivity, noting they had "softness enough to lament the loss of the ark" but lacked "hardiness enough to take one step towards the recovery of it." This serves as a rebuke to lukewarm faith.

**Futility of Idolatry**: The Dagon incident exposes the absurdity of idol worship. Henry mocks: "A sorry silly thing it was to make a god of, which, when it was down, wanted help to get up again."

**Divine Patience Limits**: The escalating judgments on the Philistines demonstrate that God's forbearance has boundaries, particularly when His honor is persistently mocked.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry suggests the Philistines placed the ark by Dagon either for religious honor or as a "trophy of victory," both approaches fundamentally misunderstanding God's nature.

**Verses 3-4**: The progressive destruction of Dagon—first falling, then losing head and hands—symbolically represents the defeat of wisdom and power. Henry notes this made Dagon "very impotent, and unworthy to be prayed to."

**Verse 5**: The threshold superstition ironically "would help to perpetuate the remembrance of Dagon's disgrace," showing how God gains honor even through pagan customs.

**Verses 6-12**: The emerods plague demonstrates escalating divine judgment. Henry emphasizes that if the Philistines had "made a good use" of Dagon's downfall and repented, "it might have prevented the vengeance."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout:
- **Psalm 76:10**: "Even the wrath of man shall praise him"
- **Isaiah 26:11**: "Lord, when thy hand is lifted up they will not see, but they shall see"
- **Psalm 78:59-65**: God awakening "as one out of sleep" to vindicate His honor
- **Malachi 1:4**: Warning against setting up what God throws down
- **2 Timothy 3:13**: Evil men becoming "worse and worse"

Henry particularly emphasizes how this narrative foreshadows Christ's ultimate victory over Satan's kingdom, making the ancient event a type of future spiritual triumph.

The commentary masterfully balances theological depth with practical application, using the Philistines' experience as both historical record and spiritual warning about the consequences of opposing God's purposes.
