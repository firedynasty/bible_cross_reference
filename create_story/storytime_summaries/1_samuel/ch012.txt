## Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Samuel 12: Summary

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents this chapter as Samuel's "farewell sermon" and "Saul's coronation sermon" to Israel's general assembly. The central theological theme is **God's faithfulness versus Israel's ingratitude**. Henry emphasizes how Israel rejected divine administration for human monarchy, noting they "cast off the divine administration" to become "like the nations in your civil government." 

A key theme is **divine sovereignty in leadership transitions**. Henry interprets Samuel's appeal about his integrity as demonstrating that godly leadership serves without corruption, contrasting Samuel's sacrificial service ("too cheap, too easy, too gentle") with the coming burden of monarchy that would take "their fields and vineyards, nay, and their very sons and daughters."

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for leaders and people:

**For Leaders:** Public servants must maintain integrity and be willing to give account. Samuel's challenge "whose ox have I taken?" exemplifies transparent leadership. Henry notes this serves multiple purposes: to "preserve his own reputation," provide "a good example" for successors, and enable confident reproof of others' sins.

**For the People:** Henry emphasizes the importance of remembering God's past faithfulness. Samuel's historical recounting serves to "reason with people" because "Religion has reason on its side." The commentary stresses that gratitude for divine benefits should prevent future rebellion.

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-5:** Henry highlights Samuel's gracious transition despite personal rejection, noting he was "old and gray-headed" yet "the more unkind to cast him off." Samuel's public accountability demonstrates that "he that will, with confidence, tell another of his sin, must see to it that he himself be clear."

**Verses 6-13:** The historical review showcases God's "righteous acts"—both "benefits he hath bestowed" and "punishments he has inflicted." Henry notes that ministers' work is to "reason with people...to convince men's judgments, and so to gain their wills and affections."

**Verses 14-15:** The blessing/curse choice emphasizes conditional covenant relationship, where obedience brings continuation while rebellion brings divine opposition.

### Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout: Psalm 80:1 (shepherd leadership), Acts 20:33 (Paul's integrity), Nehemiah 5:18 (governors not requiring support), 3 John 12 (Demetrius's good report), Judges 5:11 (God's righteous acts), and Isaiah 1:18 (reasoning together). These references reinforce themes of servant leadership, divine faithfulness, and the reasonableness of faith.

The commentary concludes with the reminder that despite Israel's demand for earthly monarchy, "The Lord is your king"—the ultimate theological assertion that human government cannot replace divine sovereignty.
