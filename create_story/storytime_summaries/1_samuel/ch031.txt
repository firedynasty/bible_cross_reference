# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 31 - Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Saul's downfall as divine judgment for accumulated sins, particularly "the blood of the Amalekites which he had sinfully spared, and that of the priests which he had more sinfully spilt." He emphasizes that **"the day of recompence has now come"** and calls readers to **"Come and see the righteous judgments of God."**

A central theological contrast emerges between trusting and forsaking God. Henry notes that while David was triumphing over the Amalekites, "the Philistines triumphing over Saul. One is set over against the other, that men may see what comes of trusting in God and what comes of forsaking him."

The commentary addresses the problem of suffering by the righteous through Jonathan's death, concluding that **"the difference between good and bad is to be made in the other world, not in this. All things come alike to all."**

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several moral lessons from Saul's suicide, condemning it as the act of "a man that had neither the fear of God nor hope in God." He contrasts Jonathan, who "died on the bed of honour" receiving his wound from enemies, with Saul who "died as a fool dieth, as a coward dieth."

The commentary warns against following evil examples, noting how Saul's suicide influenced his armor-bearer: **"by the example of his wickedness in murdering himself, he drew in his servant to be guilty of the same wickedness."**

Henry also emphasizes proper priorities in death, criticizing Saul for being concerned about his body's treatment rather than **"being solicitous to resign his soul into the hands of God who gave it."**

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Regarding Saul's wound (v. 3), Henry observes that **"destruction comes gradually upon him, and he dies so as to feel himself die"** as part of divine judgment.

On the armor-bearer's refusal to kill Saul (v. 4), Henry commends this, stating **"he did well to refuse it; for no man's servant ought to be a slave to his master's lusts or passions of any kind."**

Concerning the Philistines' treatment of Saul's body (vv. 9-10), Henry notes the irony that **"Ashtaroth, the idol that Israel had many a time gone a whoring after, now triumphs over them."**

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry references multiple scriptures including David's earlier prediction of Saul's fall (1 Sam. 26:10), Job 4:7 regarding innocent suffering, Ecclesiastes 12:7 on returning souls to God, Job 3:20-21 on longing for death, Psalm 52:5 concerning Doeg's destruction, 1 Samuel 12:25 on Israel's wickedness, and Hosea 13:10-11 about Israel's king given in anger.

The commentary demonstrates how Saul's end fulfills earlier prophetic warnings while serving as a cautionary tale about the consequences of rejecting divine authority.
