# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Zechariah 5: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Justice and Judgment**: Henry emphasizes that after visions of peace, God reveals His wrath against wickedness. He notes that "God's prophets are not only his ambassadors, to treat of peace with the sons of peace, but heralds, to proclaim war against those that delight in war." The chapter demonstrates that divine blessing doesn't exempt individual sinners from judgment.

**Universal Scope of God's Curse**: The flying roll represents God's curse extending "over the face of the whole earth," indicating that divine judgment reaches all mankind. Henry observes that "wherever sinners are, any where upon the face of the whole earth, the curse of God can and will find them out and seize them."

**Specific Sins Targeted**: God particularly condemns theft and false swearing - sins against both neighbor (second table) and God (first table). Henry emphasizes these represent broader patterns of covenant violation.

## Key Practical Applications

**Personal Accountability**: Henry warns that even amid national blessing, individual wickedness brings personal curse. He applies this directly: "Let every Israelite rejoice in the blessings of his country with trembling; for if he swear, if he steal, if he live in any course of sin, he shall see them with his eyes, but shall not have the comfort of them."

**Family Consequences**: Sin affects entire households - "It shall enter into the house of the thief and of him that swears." Henry emphasizes that God's curse "shall remain in the midst of his house" and even consume the physical structure, demonstrating sin's comprehensive destructive power.

**Need for Christ**: The vision of universal curse makes "welcome then would the tidings of a Saviour be, who came to redeem us from the curse of the law by being himself made a curse for us."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry notes the prophet's increased spiritual alertness after previous awakening, observing that "good men sometimes get by their infirmities, they make them the more careful and circumspect afterwards."

**Verses 3-4**: The curse's enforcement comes directly from God: "I will bring it forth, saith the Lord of hosts" - indicating both divine commission and power behind judgment.

**Verses 5-11**: Henry suggests this vision may prophetically address the future destruction of the Jewish nation, presented in "obscure figures and expressions, lest the plain denunciation of the second overthrow of temple and state might discourage them too much."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to broader biblical themes:
- **Ezekiel 2:9-10**: Comparing the flying roll to Ezekiel's scroll "written within and without with lamentations, and mourning, and woe"
- **Revelation 14:6**: Linking to the angel with everlasting gospel flying in heaven
- **Deuteronomy 28:17-18**: Connecting household curses to covenant violations
- **Matthew 23:32 and 1 Thessalonians 2:16**: Relating the ephah to Israel's measure of iniquity being filled

Henry masterfully weaves these connections to show how Zechariah's visions fit within Scripture's broader narrative of divine justice, human accountability, and the ultimate need for redemption through Christ.
