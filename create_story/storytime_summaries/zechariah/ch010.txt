# Matthew Henry Commentary on Zechariah Chapter 10 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as divine encouragement to post-exilic Jews, emphasizing **God's sovereignty over all circumstances**. The central theological theme is that believers must "eye the great God in all events" rather than turning to false sources of help. Henry sees prophetic significance extending beyond immediate Jewish circumstances to "the spiritual Israel of God, the gospel-church, and all true believers."

The commentary emphasizes **divine mercy and complete restoration**, highlighting God's promise that the restored people "shall be as though I had not cast them off." Henry interprets this as representing how "returning repenting sinners" are fully reconciled to God, "as if they had never been rejected."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**Prayer and Providence**: Believers must "dutifully attend the course of Providence" and "ask for mercies in their proper time," not expecting God to "go out of his usual way and method." When seeking God's blessings like seasonal rain, we should align our prayers with His ordained timing.

**Active Faith**: God's presence should motivate action, not passivity. "God's gracious presence with us to help us must not supersede, but quicken and animate, our endeavours." Believers must "work out our salvation with fear and trembling, because it is God that works in us."

**Rejecting False Comfort**: Henry warns against seeking help from "idols" or false sources, noting that "all the vanities of the heathen put together could not give rain."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry contrasts true prayer to God for rain versus consulting "teraphim" and false diviners who "have told false dreams" and "comforted in vain."

**Verse 3**: The "shepherds" represent unfaithful leaders whom God punished, while the "goats" are the wicked within the flock. God's anger was selective—the same captivity was fatherly discipline for some but judicial punishment for others.

**Verse 4**: "Out of him came forth the corner" refers to both oppressive and protective powers emanating from God's sovereignty. Henry notes that princes are "often called the corners of the people."

**Verses 5-7**: The promise that "they shall be as mighty men" represents spiritual victory, with gospel preachers who "went forth to war a good warfare" confounding worldly opposition.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive biblical connections throughout:

- **Hosea 2:21** on God hearing the heavens when they hear the earth
- **Jeremiah 14:22** on the vanities of heathen being unable to give rain  
- **1 Samuel 14:38** on princes as "corners of the people"
- **Isaiah 22:23** and **Ezra 9:8** on the "nail in the sure place"

The commentary connects New Testament themes, referencing how "God chose the weak and foolish things of the world to confound the wise and mighty" and how scattered "children of God...were by faith in Christ incorporated in the gospel-church."

Henry concludes that God's method is saving "by strengthening us," working "out our happiness by working in us to do our duty," making God both "our song and our salvation."
