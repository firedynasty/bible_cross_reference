# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Zechariah Chapter 4: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this vision as divine encouragement during Israel's temple rebuilding struggles. The **central theological message** centers on God's sovereignty and spiritual power over human limitations. The golden candlestick represents the church as God's light-bearing vessel in a dark world, while the self-sustaining oil supply from olive trees symbolizes **God's independent provision that requires no human maintenance**.

A **key interpretive principle** Henry emphasizes is that "God easily can, and often does, accomplish his gracious purposes concerning his church by his own wisdom and power, without any art or labour of man." This demonstrates God's transcendence over instrumental means while affirming His faithfulness to complete what He begins.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers facing overwhelming circumstances:

**Spiritual Dependence**: When human resources fail, believers should "leave it to God to do his work himself by his own Spirit." The temple's completion would demonstrate that divine purposes prevail despite weak allies and strong enemies.

**Humble Learning**: The commentary emphasizes that "those that would be taught of God must see and acknowledge their own ignorance" and "apply to God for instruction," as shown in Zechariah's honest confession of not understanding the vision.

**Faith in Small Beginnings**: Henry warns against despising "the day of small things," encouraging believers to trust God's timing and methods even when progress appears insignificant.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1** reveals human frailty even in spiritual encounters—Zechariah falls asleep during divine revelation, showing that "the flesh was weak" despite the willing spirit.

**Verse 6** contains the commentary's centerpiece: **"Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, saith the LORD of hosts."** Henry explains this as God's preference for "secret operations and internal influences upon the minds of men" over external force.

**Verse 7** transforms obstacles into opportunities: **"Who art thou, O great mountain? before Zerubbabel thou shalt become a plain."** Henry interprets this as God's dismissal of seemingly insurmountable difficulties, declaring that "mountains of difficulty" dissolve before divine purpose.

**Verse 10** addresses discouragement over modest progress, with Henry noting that God's "seven eyes...run to and fro through the whole earth," indicating comprehensive divine oversight.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry draws extensive scriptural connections throughout his commentary:

- **Luke 9:32** parallels Zechariah's drowsiness with the disciples' sleep during Christ's transfiguration
- **Daniel 10:9** shows similar prophetic experiences of sleep during divine encounters
- **Isaiah 50:4** connects to God's awakening work: "He wakens morning by morning, he wakens my ear"
- **Revelation 1:20** contrasts the single Jewish candlestick with seven distinct gospel churches
- **Isaiah 40:4** links mountain-leveling imagery to preparing "the way of the Lord"
- **Luke 2:46** emphasizes the importance of asking questions to understand God's mind

Henry also connects Zerubbabel typologically to Christ as both "author and finisher of our faith," ensuring that what God begins, He will certainly complete. The commentary concludes with the triumphant cry of **"Grace, grace!"**—magnifying divine favor rather than human achievement as the source of spiritual victory.
