# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Zechariah 9: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as part of "The burden of the word of the Lord," emphasizing that **"every word of God has weight in it to those who regard it, and will be a heavy weight upon those who do not."** The primary theological themes include:

**Divine Sovereignty Over Nations**: God exercises judgment not only over Israel but over all surrounding nations—Syria, Tyre, Sidon, and Philistia. Henry notes that God "is King of nations as well as King of saints; he governs the world as well as the church."

**Redemptive Judgment**: The destruction of Israel's enemies serves God's protective purposes for His people. Henry emphasizes that God's judgment on neighboring nations is "in kindness to them that God will deal thus...to avenge their quarrel for what is past and to secure them for the future."

**Universal Grace**: A remarkable theme emerges regarding Gentile conversion, where Henry interprets verse 7 as promising that "a Philistine shall be as acceptable to God, upon gospel-terms, as one of Judah...In Christ Jesus there is no distinction of nations."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**Faith and Dependence**: When "the eyes of man, as of all the tribes of Israel, are towards the Lord," it signals God's imminent intervention. This teaches believers to maintain "believing expectations from him and dependence upon him."

**Divine Protection**: God's church exists "as a lily among thorns," requiring special divine protection. Henry encourages believers that "God's power and goodness are to be observed in the special preservation" of His people.

**Wealth's Futility**: Using Tyre's example, Henry warns that earthly security fails—"there is no wisdom nor counsel against the Lord." He wishes "we could all learn so to look upon" gold and silver as worthless compared to "the merchandise of wisdom and grace."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: The "burden" on Damascus occurs when Israel turns to God in faith, demonstrating how believers' prayers trigger divine intervention.

**Verses 3-4**: Tyre's threefold security—wisdom, strength, and wealth—proves futile against divine judgment. Henry notes ironically that Tyre "thinks herself very wise, and able to outwit even the wisdom of God."

**Verses 5-6**: The Philistine cities' terror demonstrates how enemies of God's people will "be very sorrowful" when divine judgment begins.

**Verse 7**: Henry interprets this as gospel conversion, where God removes "blood and abominations" and accepts a remnant "for our God."

**Verse 8**: God's protective encampment around His house ensures "no oppressor shall pass through them any more."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to broader biblical themes:

- **John 3:36** - regarding God's abiding wrath
- **Isaiah 17:7-8** - about turning from idols to God
- **Acts 9:22** - Paul's Damascus conversion as partial fulfillment
- **Ecclesiastes 7:12** - money as defense
- **Job 27:16** - silver heaped like dust
- **Revelation 20:9; Psalm 34:7** - divine protection imagery
- **2 Samuel 24:16** - Araunah the Jebusite as example of Gentile acceptance

Henry also notes historical fulfillment through Alexander the Great's conquests and later Maccabean victories, demonstrating how prophecy finds both immediate and ultimate fulfillment in God's sovereign timeline.

The commentary masterfully weaves together themes of divine judgment, protective love, and redemptive grace, showing how God's dealings with ancient nations reveal eternal principles about His character and purposes.
