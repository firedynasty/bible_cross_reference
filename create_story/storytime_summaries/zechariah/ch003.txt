# Matthew Henry Commentary on Zechariah Chapter 3 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this vision as a profound illustration of justification and sanctification, with Joshua the high priest serving as both a representative of Israel's ecclesiastical restoration and a type of Christ. The commentary emphasizes **two central discouragements believers face before God**: "the guilt of the sins committed by us" making us "obnoxious to the justice of God," and "the power of the sin that dwells in us" making us "odious to the holiness of God."

The vision demonstrates Christ as both advocate and judge, with Henry noting: **"It is the happiness of the saints that the Judge is their friend."** Satan's accusations are countered by Christ's defense, illustrating how divine election overcomes human unworthiness. Henry interprets Joshua's "filthy garments" as representing both personal sin and the corrupted state of the priesthood, yet emphasizes God's grace in cleansing rather than rejecting.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons: When serving God, believers "must expect to meet with all the resistance that Satan's subtlety and malice can give us," but should "resist him that resists us and he shall flee from us." He emphasizes that **"Christ bears with his people, whose hearts are upright with him, and admits them into communion with himself, notwithstanding their manifold infirmities."**

The commentary stresses that God's forgiveness involves both removing guilt and empowering transformation - **"he parts between them and their sins, and so prevents their sins parting between them and their God; he reconciles himself to the sinner, but not to the sin."**

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 2**: Henry interprets "a brand plucked out of the fire" as showing Christ's compassionate advocacy, noting that narrow escapes from danger are "happy presages and powerful pleas for more eminent favours."

**Verses 3-4**: The filthy garments represent both personal corruption and institutional failure. Henry explains that Joshua's cleansing involves two works: guilt removal through "pardoning mercy" and spiritual renewal through "renewing grace."

**Verse 5**: The mitre represents restored dignity and office, accomplished through prophetic intercession.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage extensively to other biblical texts: Hebrews 7:28 (priests with infirmity), Revelation 12:10 (Satan as accuser), Revelation 1:5-6 (Christ washing believers), Revelation 19:8 (saints clothed in righteousness), Exodus 28:2 (priestly garments for glory and beauty), and Ezra 9-10 (priestly marriages to foreign women).

The commentary consistently points forward to Christ as the ultimate fulfillment, noting that both Joshua figures were "figures of him that was to come, our chief captain and our chief priest," with the vision ultimately terminating in gospel promises of justification and sanctification through Christ's work.
