# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Chronicles 21 - Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as a stark illustration of divine justice and covenant faithfulness. The central theme contrasts God's judgment on wickedness with His unwavering commitment to His promises. **"Never surely did any kingdom change its king so much for the worse as Judah did, when Jehoram, one of the vilest, succeeded Jehoshaphat, one of the best."** 

A key theological insight centers on verse 7: despite Jehoram's extreme wickedness, God preserved David's house because of His covenant promises. Henry notes: **"The tenour of the covenant was that David's seed should be visited for their transgressions, but the covenant should never be broken"** (referencing Psalm 89:30).

Henry also emphasizes the principle of divine retribution - that God's judgments often mirror the nature of sins committed. Jehoram murdered his brothers to strengthen himself, yet all his sons except one were killed, thus weakening him.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications for rulers and subjects alike:

**On Leadership Responsibility:** He questions whether Jehoshaphat acted wisely in favoring primogeniture over character, noting: **"Power is a sacred thing, with which men may either do much good or much hurt; and therefore Detur digniori--Let him that deserves it have it. Salus populi suprema lex--The security of the people is the first consideration."**

**On Political Rebellion:** When discussing Libnah's revolt, Henry suggests that subjects may justifiably resist ungodly leadership: they "could not continue subject to him without some danger of being themselves also drawn away from God and their duty."

**On Parental Influence:** Henry warns that **"it is no new thing for the children that have been most indulged by their parents to be least dutiful to them,"** showing how Jehoshaphat's generosity was repaid with evil.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-4:** Henry emphasizes the tragedy that Jehoram's brothers "were better than Jehoram, had a great deal more wisdom and virtue," yet were murdered by their own brother. He compares Jehoram's jealousy to Cain's hatred of Abel.

**Verse 6:** The influence of ungodly marriage is highlighted - Jehoram "had the daughter of Ahab to wife" and consequently "walked in the way of the house of Ahab."

**Verses 12-15:** Henry notes the significance of Elijah's written prophecy, suggesting it came before Elijah's translation and served as both a potential means of repentance and a witness against Jehoram's hardened response.

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry weaves numerous scriptural connections throughout his commentary:

- Links Jehoram's fraternal murder to **Cain and Abel**, showing the ongoing pattern of righteous blood crying out
- References **Deuteronomy 21:17** regarding birthright inheritance
- Connects to **Habakkuk 2:12** about kingdoms founded in blood
- Cites **Revelation 2:20** regarding spiritual fornication and idolatry
- Points to **1 Kings 21:27** about Ahab's temporary repentance as a missed opportunity for Jehoram

The commentary demonstrates how this historical account fits into the broader biblical narrative of God's justice, mercy, and covenant faithfulness across generations.
