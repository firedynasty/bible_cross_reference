# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Chronicles 18: Summary

## Main Theological Themes

Matthew Henry presents this chapter as a cautionary tale about compromising alliances. The central theme is **the danger of ungodly partnerships**, even for righteous people. Henry emphasizes God's sovereignty in both protecting the faithful and executing judgment on the wicked. He highlights the contrast between true and false prophecy, showing how God permits deception as judgment on those who reject truth. The commentary also underscores **divine providence**, demonstrating that "no art, no arms, can save those whom God has appointed to ruin."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed lessons for believers:

**On Bad Company**: "Those that have more wisdom, grace, and resolution, cannot be sure that they can converse familiarly with wicked people and get no hurt by them." He warns that even good men like Jehoshaphat can be corrupted through compromise.

**On Seeking God's Will**: Henry emphasizes "the great duty of acknowledging God in all our ways and enquiring at his word, whatever we undertake," noting that believers should seek God's guidance through "particular believing prayer" and "unbiased consultation of the scripture."

**On Flattery vs. Truth**: He warns against surrounding oneself with flatterers, stating that "counsel is not always best for us that is most pleasing to us."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry sees Jehoshaphat's marriage alliance with Ahab as "the worst match that ever was made by any of the house of David," driven by pride and misguided political calculation. 

Regarding Ahab's feast for Jehoshaphat, Henry observes it was "designed only to wheedle him into the expedition. The kisses of an enemy are deceitful."

On Micaiah's faithfulness versus the false prophets, Henry notes the "hard case of faithful ministers" who suffer for their truthfulness, while emphasizing Micaiah's courage in declaring, "As the LORD liveth, even what my God saith, that will I speak."

The battle's outcome reveals divine irony: "Jehoshaphat is safe in his robes, Ahab killed in his armour; for the race is not to the swift nor the battle to the strong."

## Cross-References

Henry connects this account to 1 Kings 22, noting the parallel narrative while emphasizing different perspectives on the same events. He references Psalm 26:5 and 141:4 regarding David's separation from evildoers, contrasting this with Jehoshaphat's compromise. The commentary alludes to 2 Thessalonians regarding God giving people over to "strong delusions" when they reject truth.

This chapter serves as Henry's warning against the subtle dangers of compromise and the paramount importance of maintaining spiritual discernment in all relationships and decisions.
