# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Chronicles 10: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **divine sovereignty** as the central theme, noting how God fulfills His purposes through human folly: "His counsel was thereby fulfilled. With him are strength and wisdom; both the deceived and the deceiver (the fool and the knave) are his" (Job 12:16). He stresses that while "the cause was of God, that the LORD might perform his word" (v. 15), this doesn't excuse Rehoboam's sinful behavior—God works through human actions without being responsible for their moral failures.

The commentary highlights **generational consequences of sin**, explaining how "Solomon forsakes God, and therefore not he, but his son after him, is forsaken by the greatest part of his people." Yet Henry balances this with divine faithfulness, noting that God preserves two tribes for David's sake, demonstrating how "the failings of the saints shall not frustrate any promise made to Christ their Head."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several leadership principles from Rehoboam's failure. He advocates for **humble, gentle leadership**: "Moderate counsels are generally wisest and best. Gentleness will do what violence will not do." The wise counsel to "be kind to this people, and please them, and speak good words to them" shows that "Good words cost nothing but a little self-denial, and yet they purchase good things."

He warns against pride and harsh authority, noting that "many ruin themselves and their interests by trampling upon and provoking their inferiors." Henry emphasizes that effective leadership requires wisdom and humility: "Such a wise man as Solomon may do as we will, but such a fool as Rehoboam must do as he can."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry offers nuanced observations throughout. Regarding the people's complaints (vv. 2-4), he suggests both perspectives have merit—Solomon may have become burdensome in his later years due to his apostasy, yet the people showed "turbulent and ungrateful spirits" given their peace and prosperity. He draws a spiritual parallel: unlike Solomon's potentially heavy yoke, Christ's "yoke is easy, and his burden is light."

On verse 15, he masterfully balances divine sovereignty with human responsibility, showing how God's predetermined plan doesn't diminish Rehoboam's culpability for his "haughtiness and passion."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous scriptural connections throughout. He references **Job 12:16** to explain divine sovereignty, **Job 31:13** as a model of wise leadership, and **Psalm 89:31-34** regarding God's covenant faithfulness despite chastisement. He draws parallels between Israel's rejection of Davidic rule and humanity's rejection of Christ: "We will not have this man to reign over us."

The commentary concludes with the assurance that while believers face consequences for sin, "the covenant [will] not [be] broken," anchoring hope in God's ultimate faithfulness to His promises in Christ.
