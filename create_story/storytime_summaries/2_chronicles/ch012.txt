# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Chronicles 12 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry frames this chapter around the cyclical pattern of sin, judgment, repentance, and restoration. **The central theological principle is divine retribution balanced by mercy**: "God never leaves any till they first leave him." The commentary emphasizes that prosperity often breeds spiritual complacency, noting that Rehoboam "forsook the law of the LORD" precisely when "he had established the kingdom and strengthened himself." Henry observes that "the prosperity of fools destroys them," referencing how security led to spiritual negligence.

The theme of God's corrective discipline runs throughout. Henry explains that God brought troubles "to awaken them, and recover them to repentance, before their hearts were hardened." When the people humbled themselves, declaring "The LORD is righteous," God showed mercy while still maintaining consequences to prevent future rebellion.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications about the dangers of prosperity and the necessity of humble repentance. He warns that when people prosper without spiritual discipline, "they are ready to say to God, Depart from us." The commentary emphasizes that **true repentance must be heartfelt and sustained**, not merely circumstantial.

Regarding religious commitment, Henry critiques Rehoboam's half-hearted faith: "He did evil because he prepared not his heart to seek the LORD." The practical lesson is clear: "Those are easily drawn by Satan to any evil who are wavering and inconstant in that which is good and are never persuaded to make religion their business."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On verse 8's reference to serving foreign kings, Henry provides a striking contrast: God tells the people they will "know my service, and the service of the kingdoms of the countries." He explains this as divine irony—since they complained that God's law was too burdensome, they would learn that human tyranny is far worse. **"The service of virtue is perfect liberty; the service of lust is perfect slavery."**

The golden shields replaced by bronze (verses 9-11) symbolize the spiritual and material decline resulting from abandoning God's ways.

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects this chapter to several biblical themes and passages. He references Deuteronomy 32:15 ("Jeshurun waxed fat and kicked") and Deuteronomy 28:47-48 regarding serving enemies "in hunger and thirst." The commentary also alludes to Hosea 2:7 about returning to one's "first Master" and Ezekiel 20:24-25 concerning God giving "statutes that were not good" as judgment.

Henry draws parallels to the book of Judges' cycle of apostasy and restoration, suggesting this pattern continues in the monarchical period. The emphasis on Jerusalem as "the city which the LORD had chosen" underscores both privilege and responsibility, making Rehoboam's unfaithfulness more egregious.
