# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Chronicles 32 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Sennacherib's invasion as divinely orchestrated to demonstrate God's power on behalf of His reformed people. He emphasizes that trials often follow spiritual reformation, noting that the invasion came "After these things, and the establishment thereof" - precisely when one might expect peace. Henry argues this timing served three purposes: it allowed reformation to be completed first, gave God opportunity to show Himself strong for His people, and provided occasion for divine deliverance that would bring honor to both God and Hezekiah.

The central theological theme contrasts human versus divine power. Henry highlights Hezekiah's declaration: "With him is an arm of flesh; but with us is the LORD our God to help us, and to fight our battles." This represents the fundamental choice between trusting earthly might or divine providence.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Balance of Faith and Works**: "Those that trust God with their safety must yet use proper means for their safety, otherwise they tempt him, and do not trust him. God will provide, but so must we also." Hezekiah's military preparations exemplify faithful pragmatism.

**Conquering Fear through Faith**: Henry emphasizes that "a believing confidence in God will raise us above the prevailing fear of man," encouraging Christians to "rest upon his word, and boldly say, Since God is for us, who can be against us?"

**Leadership in Crisis**: Hezekiah models godly leadership by speaking "comfortably" to his people, putting "life into his people" through encouragement rather than panic.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides rich exposition on Hezekiah's defensive strategy (verses 3-6), explaining how stopping water sources was practical military wisdom - "A powerful army, if it want water but a few days, will be but a heap of dry dust."

On Sennacherib's blasphemy (verses 13-19), Henry notes the escalating impiety: the king's servants "spoke yet more against the LORD God" than even their blasphemous master commanded, showing how "servants learned insolence from their master."

The divine response (verses 20-21) receives brief but powerful treatment, emphasizing that God "sent an angel" to accomplish what human armies could not.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this account to related passages: 2 Kings 18-19 for the fuller narrative, Isaiah 7:2 comparing Hezekiah's faith to his father Ahaz's fearfulness, Isaiah 10:24 for prophetic encouragement, Isaiah 33:14-18 for the righteous dwelling securely while "sinners in Zion were afraid," Isaiah 51:12-13 on forgetting God while fearing oppressors, and Psalms 11:1 and 42:10 regarding enemies attempting to undermine trust in God.

Henry concludes by noting that this deliverance enhanced Hezekiah's reputation internationally, as "many brought gifts unto the LORD to Jerusalem, and presents to Hezekiah," demonstrating how God's faithfulness to His people becomes a testimony to the nations.
