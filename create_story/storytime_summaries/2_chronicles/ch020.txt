# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Chronicles 20: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry identifies this chapter as a masterpiece of divine providence, demonstrating how God rewards piety with "marvellous loving-kindness." The central theological theme is complete dependence upon God in crisis. He emphasizes that "if we meet with trouble in the way of duty, we may believe it is that God may have an opportunity of showing us so much the more of his marvellous loving-kindness."

The commentary highlights God's sovereign dominion over all nations and His faithfulness to covenant relationships. Henry stresses that "the battle is not yours, but God's," showing how human extremity becomes God's opportunity to display His power and faithfulness.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers facing crises:

**Proper Response to Danger**: When threatened, believers should first "set themselves to seek the Lord" rather than immediately relying on human resources. Jehoshaphat's response models how "those that would seek the Lord so as to find him...must set themselves to seek him, must do it with fixedness of thought, with sincerity of intention, and with the utmost vigour."

**The Role of Fasting and Prayer**: Henry advocates for public fasting as both confession ("we own ourselves unworthy of the bread we eat") and commitment ("fasting for sin implies a resolution to fast from it"). Magistrates should call people to such observances for national crises.

**Complete Dependence on God**: The ultimate practical lesson is Jehoshaphat's confession: "we have no might against this great company...neither know we what to do: but our eyes are upon thee."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry particularly emphasizes Jehoshaphat's prayer structure, noting five key elements: acknowledging God's sovereign dominion, claiming covenant relationship, asserting legitimate possession of the land, appealing to the sanctuary's significance, and pleading enemy injustice.

On verse 17's promise "Ye shall not need to fight," Henry connects this to Moses at the Red Sea, showing how God sometimes calls His people to be "joyful spectators" rather than "glorious instruments" of deliverance.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively cross-references scripture throughout: Psalm 83:6-8 regarding the confederacy against Israel, Hebrews 11:7 on holy fear spurring preparation, Jonah 4:11 about God's mercy toward children, James 2:23 on Abraham as God's friend, Romans 11:28 on being "beloved for the father's sake," and Exodus 14:13 paralleling Moses' encouragement at the Red Sea.

The commentary demonstrates Henry's method of letting scripture interpret scripture, showing how this historical account illuminates broader biblical principles about faith, prayer, and divine deliverance that apply across all ages.
