## Matthew Henry on 2 Chronicles 29: Hezekiah's Reformation

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Hezekiah's reign as a "pleasant scene" marked by exceptional religious devotion. The primary theological theme centers on **covenant renewal and divine reconciliation**. Hezekiah's declaration—"It is in my heart to make a covenant with the Lord God of Israel, that his fierce wrath may turn away from us"—reflects Henry's emphasis that national calamity stems from spiritual neglect, and restoration comes through renewed commitment to God's ordinances.

Henry emphasizes the **primacy of spiritual reform over political concerns**: "Those that begin with God begin at the right end of their work, and it will prosper accordingly." This theological principle undergirds the entire chapter's interpretation.

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for contemporary believers:

**Personal sanctification precedes public service**: The Levites must "sanctify themselves first" before sanctifying the temple. Henry stresses that religious leaders cannot effectively minister without personal spiritual renewal.

**Urgency in spiritual matters**: Hezekiah's immediate action upon taking the throne demonstrates that "those that do good work learn to rid work and get it done." Henry warns against spiritual negligence, noting that "men's negligence in religion is owing to their carnal security."

**Faithful service despite difficult circumstances**: Even when "the vessels of the sanctuary may be profaned for a while, God will find a time and a way to sanctify them."

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry highlights Hezekiah's **mature leadership** at age 25, contrasting him with younger reforming kings whose youth delayed reformation. He notes Hezekiah's emotional preparation: "His soul no doubt wept in secret" over his father's idolatry.

Regarding the temple cleansing, Henry emphasizes both **physical and spiritual pollution**—noting that idols were "a greater pollution" than common dirt. The **sixteen-day cleansing process** symbolizes systematic spiritual renewal beginning each new year.

The **collaborative effort** between priests and Levites demonstrates proper ecclesiastical order while maintaining "usefulness" without forgetting "their place."

### Cross-References and Scriptural Connections

Henry connects Hezekiah's character to **David's legacy**, noting this king possessed "as hearty an affection for the ark and law of God as ever David had"—unlike previous kings who did right but "not like David."

He references **2 Kings 16:15** regarding the compromised priesthood under Ahaz, and draws parallels between the temple's **lamp-lighting and incense-burning** to contemporary neglect of Scripture reading and prayer.

The cleansing process echoes **annual spiritual renewal**, with Henry advocating that "every year begin with the reformation of what is amiss, and the purging away, by true repentance, of all the defilements contracted the foregoing year."

Henry's commentary presents Hezekiah as an exemplary reformer whose immediate, comprehensive spiritual renewal demonstrates the proper ordering of priorities and the urgency required in matters of faith and worship.
