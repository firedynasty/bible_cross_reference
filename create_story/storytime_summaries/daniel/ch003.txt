# Matthew Henry Commentary on Daniel Chapter 3: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry identifies this narrative as "a glorious instance of the power and goodness of God" that demonstrates how true honor comes from faithfulness to God rather than worldly position. The three Hebrew men were "more truly, more highly, honoured by their God" through suffering than through their princely status. Henry emphasizes the contrast between temporary earthly honor and eternal divine approval, noting that "the saints' honour is reserved for another world."

The commentary highlights the spiritual danger of incomplete conversion, observing how Nebuchadnezzar's earlier "strong convictions often come short of a sound conversion." Despite acknowledging Israel's God, the king's pride transformed his dream interpretation into defiance—where he once humbly worshipped God, he now "set up for a bold competitor with God."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers facing pressure to compromise their faith:

**Faithful Witness in High Places**: The Hebrew princes exemplified how believers in positions of authority must "stand up against" idolatry, not merely avoid it privately. They attended the assembly to "bear a public testimony against this gross idolatry."

**Responding to Persecution**: Their response—"we are not careful to answer thee in this matter"—demonstrates confident trust in God's sovereignty. Henry emphasizes their absolute commitment: "But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods."

**Worldly Pressures**: The commentary warns about dual temptations facing believers: "the charms of music to allure them" and "the terrors of the fiery furnace to frighten them." Henry notes that "sense directs" most people toward compromise.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-7**: Henry suggests Nebuchadnezzar's golden image represented his attempt to be "the whole image" rather than just the golden head from his dream, showing how "his mind rising with his condition" led to prideful rebellion against God's revealed plan.

**Verses 16-18**: The Hebrew princes' famous declaration receives particular attention. Henry emphasizes their unwavering faith regardless of outcome—trusting God's ability to deliver while remaining committed even "if not."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this account to **Hebrews 11:34**, noting the apostle's reference to those "who by faith quenched the violence of fire" among the heroes of faith. He also references **Isaiah 46:6** regarding idolaters who "lavish gold out of the bag," contrasting pagan devotion with Christian "niggardliness in the worship of the true God."

The commentary references **Jeremiah 18:20** when discussing how the Chaldeans repaid good with evil, and draws parallels to kings bringing presents to Christ, showing how earthly honor should point toward divine worship.

Henry frames this story as an encouragement "to the constancy of his people in trying times," demonstrating that God honors those who choose to "rather suffer than to sin."
