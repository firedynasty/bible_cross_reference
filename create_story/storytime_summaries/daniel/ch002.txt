# Matthew Henry Commentary on Daniel 2: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry interprets Daniel 2 as prophetic history that "look much higher, to the four monarchies, and the concerns of Israel in them, and the kingdom of the Messiah, which should be set up in the world upon the ruins of them." This elevates the narrative beyond mere court intrigue to cosmic significance regarding God's sovereign plan.

A central theme is **divine omniscience versus human limitation**. Henry notes how the Chaldean magicians confess "there is none other that can show it before the king, except the gods, whose dwelling is not with flesh." He observes this as "an instance of the ignorance of these magicians" regarding polytheism, yet acknowledges their recognition that only divine beings possess perfect knowledge of human thoughts - a truth that "even the light of nature teaches."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons about **God's sovereignty over earthly powers**. He notes that "Nebuchadnezzar was a troubler of God's Israel, but God here troubled him; for he that made the soul can make his sword to approach to it." Even the mightiest rulers cannot escape God's reach.

Regarding **material prosperity and inner peace**, Henry observes: "We look into their houses, and are tempted to envy them; but, could we look into their hearts, we should pity them rather." He contrasts worldly anxiety with divine rest: "But God gives his beloved sleep, who return to him as their rest."

Henry also addresses **the danger of unchecked authority**, noting how "arbitrary power is deaf to reason" when describing Nebuchadnezzar's unreasonable demands.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry addresses the chronological difficulty in verse 1's "second year" by suggesting Daniel's exceptional advancement or alternative dating systems, preferring early dating because "Daniel was famous both for wisdom and prevalence in prayer long before" Nebuchadnezzar's later conquests.

On verse 11's confession by the magicians, Henry sees divine irony: this acknowledgment of divine omniscience serves "to the honour of God and their own condemnation, who though they knew there is a God in heaven... yet offered up their prayers and praises to dumb idols."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this account to **Joseph's interpretation of Pharaoh's dreams**, noting parallel divine elevation of Hebrew captives in foreign courts. He references **Ecclesiastes 5:7** regarding "multitude of dreams" and their vanities.

He cites **Amos 4:13** ("God understands our thoughts afar off") and **Psalm 139:2** regarding God's omniscience. The commentary also references **Jeremiah 10:11** to explain the Aramaic language shift in Daniel.

Henry's interpretation positions Daniel 2 as both historical narrative and prophetic blueprint, demonstrating God's sovereignty over human kingdoms while preparing for Messianic fulfillment. The chapter serves as a bridge between immediate political concerns and eternal spiritual truths, showing how God uses even pagan rulers to accomplish His purposes and honor His servants.
