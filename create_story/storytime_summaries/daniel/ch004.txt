# Matthew Henry Commentary on Daniel Chapter 4 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes that Daniel 4 represents **God's absolute sovereignty over earthly rulers**, with Nebuchadnezzar serving as the ultimate example of divine supremacy conquering human pride. The commentary presents this as "a lasting proof of God's supremacy, a monument of his glory, a trophy of his victory, and a warning to all not to think of prospering while they lift up or harden their hearts against God."

The chapter demonstrates **God's providential control** through both judgment and mercy, showing how divine revelation works alongside divine providence. Henry notes that God gave fair warning before judgment, allowing opportunity for repentance, yet accomplished His purposes when that warning was ignored.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from Nebuchadnezzar's experience:

**Public testimony of God's dealings:** "We ought to show to others God's dealings with us, both the rebukes we have been under and the favours we have received." Even when such testimony brings personal shame, believers should "give glory to God, not only by praising him for his mercies, but by confessing our sins."

**Security versus divine sovereignty:** God can reach the greatest people "with his terrors even when they are most secure." This serves as a warning against presumptuous confidence in earthly prosperity and power.

**Recognition of divine kingdom:** Henry emphasizes acknowledging "God's dominion" and that "His kingdom is an everlasting kingdom" - unlike temporary human kingdoms that are "confined to one generation."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry observes that Nebuchadnezzar's royal style (v.1) is remarkably "plain, short, and unaffected" rather than using "great swelling words of vanity," showing how his recent humiliation had genuinely changed him.

Regarding the dream's timing (v.4), Henry notes it came precisely when Nebuchadnezzar was "at rest in his house, and flourishing" after completing his conquests - demonstrating God's ability to disturb even the most secure.

The magicians' failure (vv.6-7) fulfilled Isaiah's prophecy about Babylon's sorcerers being unable to help in the time of need, while setting up Daniel's interpretation to bring greater glory to Israel's God.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this narrative to several biblical passages: **Ezekiel 31:3ff** (comparing the Assyrian to a cut-down tree), **Psalm 118:23** and **66:2** (marveling at God's works), **Daniel 2:44** (the everlasting kingdom), and **Isaiah 47:12-13** (failure of Babylonian astrology). These connections show how Nebuchadnezzar's experience fits within broader biblical themes of God's judgment on proud nations and the establishment of His eternal kingdom.

The commentary presents this chapter as both historical record and prophetic warning, demonstrating that "there is a divine revelation in the world, as well as a divine Providence."
