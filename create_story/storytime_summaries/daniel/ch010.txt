# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Daniel Chapter 10: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry identifies this chapter as introducing a prophetic vision delivered through "express words" rather than symbolic imagery. The **central theological theme** is Christ's pre-incarnate appearance as "the eternal Word," evidenced by similarities to Revelation 1:13-15. Henry emphasizes the **condescension of Christ**, noting how "over all this splendor he drew a veil when he took upon him the form of a servant." 

The commentary highlights **divine revelation as progressive**, distinguishing between general revelation available to all and special visions given to God's favorites. Henry notes that "there is a divine revelation vouchsafed to all...but such a vision must be peculiar to Daniel, who was a favourite."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from Daniel's experience:

**On fasting and self-denial**: Despite Daniel's age and status, "when it was both to testify and to assist his devotion, he could thus deny himself." This serves as rebuke to "young people in the common ranks of life who cannot persuade themselves thus to deny themselves."

**On spiritual mourning**: Henry commends how "good men cannot but mourn to see how slowly the work of God goes on in the world and what opposition it meets with." Daniel's three-week mourning demonstrates proper concern for God's people and purposes.

**On reverence balanced with accessibility**: While acknowledging that "no man can see [divine glory] and live," Henry warns against letting "reverence of God's glory...degenerate into such a dread of him as will disable or indispose us to hear it."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides rich details on Christ's appearance (verses 5-6), explaining the **priestly significance** of the linen clothing and golden girdle, and the **beryl body** as "precious stone of a sky-colour." The **lightning face** "dazzles the eyes, both brightens and threatens."

Regarding verse 13's reference to "the prince of the kingdom of Persia," Henry interprets this as spiritual warfare, where even divine messengers face opposition from demonic forces governing earthly kingdoms.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to **Revelation 1:13-15** to establish Christ's identity. He references **Acts 9:7 and 22:9** regarding Paul's companions who experienced supernatural phenomena without seeing the vision directly. 

The commentary links Daniel's prostration to **Moses' fear** ("I exceedingly fear and quake") and connects the divine voice's pacifying effect to **John 14:22** about Christ manifesting himself to believers "but not to the world."

Henry also references **Ezra 4:4-5** regarding opposition to temple rebuilding as potential context for Daniel's mourning, demonstrating how this vision addresses contemporary concerns while revealing future events extending "as far forward as to the end of the world and the resurrection of the dead."
