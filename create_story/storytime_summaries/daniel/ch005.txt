# Matthew Henry Commentary on Daniel 5: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Daniel 5 as a powerful demonstration of divine justice and the futility of human pride against God's sovereignty. The chapter illustrates three key theological principles: **God's judgment on sacrilege and blasphemy**, **the insufficiency of worldly wisdom against divine revelation**, and **the certainty of divine retribution**. Henry emphasizes that Belshazzar's feast represents the culmination of Babylon's iniquity, noting how "security and sensuality are sad presages of approaching ruin."

The mysterious handwriting serves as God's final warning—a divine arrest warrant written by "the finger of God." Henry connects this to other biblical instances where God writes directly (the tablets of Moses), showing God's consistent pattern of communicating judgment through His written word.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications for believers:

**Against presumptuous security**: Belshazzar's feast during a siege demonstrates dangerous complacency. "Those that will not be warned by judgments of God may expect to be wounded by them."

**On the power of conscience**: The king's immediate terror shows how "God can soon awaken the most secure and make the heart of the stoutest sinner to tremble; and there needs no more to do it than to let loose his own thoughts upon him."

**Regarding sacred things**: Henry warns that "mirth is sinful indeed...which profanes sacred things and jests with them," emphasizing God's jealousy for His honor.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-4**: Henry sees Belshazzar's feast as a threefold affront to God—against His providence (feasting during judgment), His sanctuary (desecrating temple vessels), and His deity (praising idols). The use of holy vessels wasn't mere frivolity but "malicious despite to the God of Israel."

**Verses 5-9**: The supernatural handwriting represents divine communication that earthly wisdom cannot interpret. Henry notes the irony that "drunken worshippers...are the most proper for the service of dunghill deities."

The king's physical response—changed countenance, loosened joints, knocking knees—demonstrates how "his own guilty conscience flew in his face."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout his commentary:

- **Isaiah 22:12-13**: Contrasting God's call to mourning with Belshazzar's feasting
- **Isaiah 21:2-4**: "The night of my pleasures has he turned into fear to me"
- **Psalm 137:3**: Babylon's mockery of "the songs of Zion"
- **Job 26:14**: "These are parts of his ways" regarding partial revelation of God's power

Henry also references the historical context of Cyrus's siege and connects Daniel's interpretation to God's pattern of using His servants to reveal truth when worldly wisdom fails. The commentary emphasizes that while human interpreters failed, God reserved "the honour of expounding this mystical writing...for Daniel," demonstrating that divine revelation requires divine illumination.
