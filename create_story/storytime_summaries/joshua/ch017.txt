# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Joshua 17: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **God's sovereignty in inheritance** through the detailed tribal divisions, particularly highlighting how "God so ordering it" ensured tribes would "intermix one with another, to keep up mutual acquaintance and correspondence." The daughters of Zelophehad represent **divine justice and provision**, with Henry noting their "pious zeal and prudent forecast" as an example that "those who take care in the wilderness of this world to make sure to themselves a place in the inheritance of the saints in light will certainly have the comfort of it."

The commentary stresses **divine blessing and human responsibility**, particularly regarding population growth where the tribes acknowledge "the Lord has blessed me hitherto." However, Henry emphasizes uncertainty about future blessings, noting their wisdom in not speaking "confidently of the continuance" of God's favor.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications about **contentment versus complaint**. The children of Joseph become "blamers of their lot," wanting favor due to Joshua's tribal connection. Henry commends Joshua's **impartial leadership**: "in the discharge of his office, as a public person, he had no more regard to his own tribe than to any other."

The core practical lesson centers on **work and warfare over ease**. When the tribes complain about their inheritance, Joshua responds that they must "work and fight" rather than "indulge themselves in ease and luxury." Henry applies this broadly: "Many wish for larger possessions who do not cultivate and make the best of what they have" and "Most people's poverty is the effect of their idleness; would they dig, they need not beg."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Regarding Machir's inheritance (v.1), Henry notes he "signalized himself as a man of war" and his "warlike disposition descended to his posterity," explaining why they received land beyond Jordan.

On the daughters of Zelophehad (vv.3-6), Henry observes they "seasonably put in their demand themselves" rather than through husbands, and Joshua readily honored God's previous command without objecting that they "having not served in the wars of Canaan" shouldn't share in its possessions.

Concerning the Canaanites remaining (vv.12-13), Henry criticizes how they "suffered the Canaanites to live among them, contrary to the command of God, serving their own ends by conniving at them."

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects this passage to **Numbers 26:30-32** and **Numbers 36:12** regarding tribal genealogies and inheritance laws. He references **1 Chronicles 7:21** concerning Machir's military background and **1 Chronicles 5:24** for family heads.

The commentary links to **Jude 16** regarding complainers being "blamers of their lot." Henry also connects to the broader Genesis narrative, noting that though Manasseh "was the first-born," Jacob had "preferred Ephraim before him."

The theological application draws from the **inheritance of the saints** concept, creating a typological connection between earthly inheritance in Canaan and eternal inheritance. Henry concludes with the challenge: "What can be insuperable to faith and holy resolution?"
