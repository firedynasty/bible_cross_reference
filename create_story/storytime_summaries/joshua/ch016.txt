# Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on Joshua Chapter 16

## **Main Theological Themes and Interpretations**

Matthew Henry emphasizes God's providential blessing upon Joseph's descendants, noting that though Joseph was among Jacob's younger sons, "he was his eldest by his most just and best beloved wife Rachel" and "had been the greatest ornament and support of his family." The inheritance of Ephraim and Manasseh reflects divine favor, as their portion lay "in the very heart of the land of Canaan" and "took up the whole breadth of Canaan from side to side."

Henry highlights God's sovereignty in the rise and fall of families, observing that "Families and kingdoms are diminished and increased, increased and diminished again, as God pleases." He notes how the numerical positions of Ephraim and Manasseh reversed over time, fulfilling Moses' blessing that distinguished between "the ten thousands of Ephraim and the thousands of Manasseh."

## **Key Practical Applications**

The commentary strongly condemns Ephraim's failure to completely drive out the Canaanites from Gezer, attributing this to "either carelessness or cowardice, either for want of faith in the promise of God" or "want of zeal for the command of God." Henry warns against compromise with ungodliness, suggesting the Ephraimites spared the Canaanites "out of covetousness" and by dealing with them were "in danger of being infected with their idolatry."

He emphasizes the importance of complete obedience to God's commands rather than seeking comfortable compromises that might appear to satisfy divine requirements while actually undermining spiritual integrity.

## **Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights**

Henry acknowledges the difficulty in precisely mapping the territorial boundaries described, noting that "the learned, who aim to be exact in drawing the line according to the directions here, find themselves very much at a loss." He observes that geographical changes over time make reconstruction challenging, as "cities have been so destroyed as that no mark nor footstep of them remains, but brooks are dried up, rivers alter their courses."

Regarding the separate cities given to Ephraim within Manasseh's territory (verse 9), Henry suggests this arrangement worked because both tribes were "sons of Joseph, and should love as brethren," demonstrating practical wisdom in tribal allocation.

## **Cross-References to Other Scripture**

Henry connects this inheritance to the patriarchal blessings in Genesis 49:25-26 and Deuteronomy 33:13, showing how the land's fruitfulness fulfilled earlier promises. He references the tribal census in Numbers 26:34, 37 and Moses' blessing in Deuteronomy 33:17 to illustrate changing tribal populations.

The commentary also notes significant locations within Ephraim's territory that feature prominently in later biblical history, including Shiloh (where the tabernacle was established), Samaria (capital of the northern kingdom), and places associated with Christ's ministry like Sychar and Jacob's well (John 11:54). Henry observes that "the whole kingdom of the ten tribes is often, in the prophets, especially in Hosea, called Ephraim."
