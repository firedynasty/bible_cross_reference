# Matthew Henry Commentary on Joshua 11: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes God's sovereignty in Israel's conquest, particularly highlighting divine hardening of hearts as a theme of divine justice. He notes that "it was of the LORD to harden their hearts, that they should come against Israel in battle, that he might destroy them utterly" (v. 20). This demonstrates that "sinners bring ruin upon their own heads, so that God will be justified when he speaks."

The commentary draws parallels between the conquest and spiritual warfare, observing that "the war carried on by the preaching of the gospel against Satan's kingdom was at first forwarded by miracles" but believers must now rely on "ordinary assistance of divine grace in the use of the sword of the Spirit."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Unity Against Common Enemies**: The Canaanite confederation teaches that "the children of this world are more unanimous, and therein wiser, than the children of light. The oneness of the church's enemies should shame the church's friends out of their discords and divisions."

**Trust in God Over Military Might**: Joshua's destruction of horses and chariots demonstrates "confidence in the power of God engaged for Israel, which enabled them to despise the chariots and horses which others trusted in" (Psalm 20:7; 33:17). This was "cutting off a right hand" to prevent misplaced trust.

**Courage Through God's Word**: Even Joshua, "remarkable for his courage," needed repeated encouragement, showing that "fresh dangers and difficulties make it necessary to fetch in fresh supports and comforts from the word of God."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-5**: Henry notes God's wisdom in allowing enemies to confederate separately rather than all at once, "preparing his people to encounter the greater force, by breaking the less."

**Verse 6**: God's promise "tomorrow about this time" shows divine precision in timing, and believers should "keep time with God."

**Verse 15**: Henry emphasizes Joshua's complete obedience: "he left nothing undone of all that the LORD commanded Moses," demonstrating faithful transmission of divine commands through generations.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects the narrative to broader biblical themes:
- **Genesis 49:9** - Judah as a lion that others rouse "at their peril"
- **Romans 15:16** - The Canaanites rejecting grace were "made sacrifices to his justice"
- **Luke 23:12** - Former enemies uniting against a common threat
- **Deuteronomy 6:10** - Israel inheriting "great and goodly cities which they builded not"

The commentary concludes that just as Israel was "enriched with the spoil of their oppressors when they came out of Egypt," now they gained wealth to "set up in the land of Canaan," fulfilling the principle that "the wealth of the sinner laid up for the just."
