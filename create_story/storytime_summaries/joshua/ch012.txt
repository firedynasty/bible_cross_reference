## Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on Joshua 12

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Joshua 12 as a divine summary of God's faithfulness in fulfilling His promises to Israel. The chapter serves as both a conclusion to the conquest narratives and a preface to the land distribution that follows. He emphasizes the **continuity of God's work** through different leaders, noting that "God was the same who wrought both" under Moses and Joshua, proclaiming Him "the Alpha and Omega of Israel's great salvation."

A key theological theme is the **typological relationship between Joshua and Christ**. Henry draws a parallel: "Moses, in his time, gave to one part of Israel...external temporal blessings...but our Lord Jesus, the true Joshua, has provided for all the children of promise spiritual blessings—the privileges of the sanctuary, and the heavenly Canaan."

### Key Practical Applications

Henry emphasizes several practical lessons:

1. **Remembering Past Mercies**: "Fresh mercies must not drown the remembrance of former mercies." Believers should honor previous faithful servants while celebrating present blessings.

2. **Recognizing God's Goodness**: The thirty-one conquered kingdoms demonstrate "how good God was to Israel" and "what obligations he hereby laid upon them to observe his statutes and to keep his laws."

3. **Understanding Divine Judgment**: The current barren state of the land serves as a warning—it demonstrates "the effect of the curse it lies under, since its possessors rejected Christ and his gospel."

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides detailed geographical descriptions of the conquered territories. He notes that King Og "had palaces in both" Ashtaroth and Edrei, "one perhaps was his summer seat and the other his winter seat," but observes that "Israel took both from him, and made one grave to serve him that could not be content with one palace."

Regarding the land's diversity (v. 8), Henry describes "mountains, not craggy, and rocky, and barren...but fruitful hills" and "valleys, not mossy and boggy, but covered with corn."

Henry addresses the missing Girgashites among the seven nations, suggesting they either "incorporated with some other of these nations" or "withdrew and went into Africa" rather than fight Israel.

### Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to other biblical texts:
- Numbers 21:24 and Numbers 32 for the original conquest accounts
- Deuteronomy 33:15 describing fruitful hills
- Psalm 65:13 about valleys covered with corn  
- Deuteronomy 29:23 regarding the land's future curse
- Psalm 105:44-45 and Psalm 136:17 celebrating God's victories and enduring mercy

The commentary concludes by affirming that these conquered kings fulfilled the psalm's declaration: "the great and famous kings that God smote, for his mercy endureth for ever; and gave their land for a heritage, even a heritage unto Israel his servant."
