## Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on Joshua Chapter 20

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry views the cities of refuge primarily as **typological prefigurations of Christ**. He emphasizes that "this remedial law was a figure of good things to come," seeing these sanctuaries as symbols of "the relief which the gospel provides for poor penitent sinners, and their protection from the curse of the law and the wrath of God, in our Lord Jesus, to whom believers flee for refuge." The cities represent both God's justice (protecting the innocent) and His mercy (providing sanctuary for the accidentally guilty).

Henry also highlights God's providential care, noting how God provided "not only for their ease at all times, but for their safety in times of danger." The commentary emphasizes that God's spiritual Israel has "in Christ and heaven, not only rest to repose themselves in, but refuge to secure themselves in."

### Key Practical Applications

The commentary draws several practical lessons:
- **Universal need for refuge**: Since "no man could be sure but some time or other it might be his own case," everyone should appreciate these provisions
- **Preparation for danger**: "Such times we must expect and prepare for in this world"  
- **Accessibility of protection**: The cities were strategically located so "a man might in half a day reach some one of them from any corner of the country"
- **Divine justice balanced with mercy**: Protection for those whose "hand only was guilty but not his heart"

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry notes the careful legal process (verses 4-6): the refugee must "declare his cause in the ears of the elders" and remain until "the death of the high priest." He explains this confinement as being "prisoner at large" - protected but restricted.

Regarding the cities' locations (verses 7-9), Henry observes their strategic placement across three regions and notes they were **"all Levites' cities,"** providing both honor to God's tribe and spiritual care for refugees who "had the servants of God's house with him, to instruct him, and pray for him."

### Cross-References and Symbolic Meanings

Henry connects the cities to **Hebrews 6:18** (believers fleeing to Christ for refuge), **Philippians 3:9** (being found in Christ), and **Romans 8:1** (no condemnation). He finds significance in the city names: "Kedesh signifies holy, and our refuge is the holy Jesus. Shechem, a shoulder, and the government is upon his shoulder. Hebron, fellowship, and believers are called into the fellowship of Christ Jesus."

The commentary concludes by noting that even "the horns of the altar" served as refuge, with **Christ as "our altar, who not only sanctifies the gift, but protects the giver."**
