# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Joshua 9: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as a study in divine sovereignty and human response to God's power. The **central theological tension** lies between the Canaanite kings who "had their minds blinded, and their hearts hardened to their destruction" versus the Gibeonites who demonstrated faith by submitting to Israel's God. Henry emphasizes that "the discovery of the glory and grace of God in the gospel is to some a savour of life unto life, but to others a savour of death unto death" (2 Cor. 2:16), illustrating how the same divine revelation produces opposite responses.

The commentary highlights **God's providence in judgment and mercy**—while the other kings foolishly confederated against Israel despite witnessing God's power, the Gibeonites wisely sought peace, recognizing they "durst not contend with Israel, who had an Almighty God on their side."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Spiritual warfare vigilance**: "In our spiritual warfare we must stand against the wiles of the devil, remembering he is a subtle serpent as well as a roaring lion." Joshua needed to guard "against secret fraud as well as against open force."

**Unity against common enemies**: Henry urges that "Israel would learn this of Canaanites, to sacrifice private interests to the public welfare, and to lay aside all animosities among themselves, that they may cordially unite against the common enemies of God's kingdom."

**Careful decision-making**: "In all leagues of relation and friendship we must first try and then trust, lest we repent at leisure agreements made in haste."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On **verse 14** ("asked not counsel at the mouth of the LORD"), Henry identifies this as the critical failure—Israel's leaders acted on physical evidence alone without seeking divine guidance.

Regarding the Gibeonites' **deception** (verses 4-13), Henry notes the moral complexity: "Their falsehood cannot be justified," yet "their faith and prudence are to be greatly commended" for submitting to Israel's God, which "implied a renunciation of the god they had served."

Henry observes that **one lie necessitates another**: "when they had once said, We have come from a far country, they found themselves necessitated to say it again...for one lie is an inlet to another, and that to a third, and so on."

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects this narrative to **Luke 14:32** regarding wisdom in making peace when facing superior forces. He references **Luke 16:8** to commend the Gibeonites' prudence despite their deception, noting how Christ "commended even the unjust steward, because he had done wisely."

The commentary links to **2 Corinthians 2:16** to explain differing responses to God's revelation, and **Deuteronomy 7:1-3** and **20:10,15** to explain the legal framework that motivated the Gibeonites' deception.

Henry also draws parallels to modern charity fraud, noting how "there are those who make themselves poor with the badges of want and distress" (**Proverbs 13:7**), warning that "charity is misplaced and diverted from those that are real objects of it."
