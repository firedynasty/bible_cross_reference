# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Joshua 6: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Joshua 6 as a divine contest between God's sovereignty and human defiance. **Central themes include:**

**Divine Sovereignty vs. Human Opposition:** Henry contrasts Jericho's resolution that "Israel shall not be its master" with God's counter-resolution that "Israel shall be its master, and that quickly." He emphasizes God's assured victory, noting the past tense in verse 2: "See, I have given into thy hand Jericho" - highlighting that divine victory is already accomplished.

**Faith Over Force:** The commentary emphasizes that "by faith, not by force, the walls of Jericho fell down," connecting to Hebrews 11:30. God's unusual military strategy demonstrates that "the excellency of the power might be of God."

**The Ark's Centrality:** Henry sees God's choice to center the victory around the ark as magnifying divine power while honoring "the instituted token of his presence" and putting "honour upon the priests."

## Key Practical Applications

**Patient Obedience:** Henry draws extensive application about waiting on God's timing: "promised deliverances must be expected in God's way, so they must be expected in his time. He that believes does not make haste." The thirteen circuits before victory teach that believers must wait "patiently for the Lord."

**Ministry Parallels:** He applies the priests' trumpet-blowing to Christian ministers who must "blow the trumpet in Zion" by "denouncing war against all those that go on still in their trespasses" while also "encouraging the good soldiers of Jesus Christ" through "the Jubilee trumpet of the everlasting gospel."

**Spiritual Warfare:** The rams' horn trumpets become a metaphor for "the foolishness of preaching" and "the weapons of our warfare" that are "mighty through God to the pulling down of strong-holds" (2 Cor. 10:4-5).

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1:** Henry notes Jericho was "infatuated" with "hearts hardened to their own destruction" - representing "all those that strengthen themselves against the Almighty" (Job 15:25).

**Verses 3-5:** The unusual siege strategy served multiple purposes: magnifying God's power, honoring the ark and priests, testing Israel's "faith, obedience, and patience," and encouraging hope for future conquests.

**Verse 10:** The commanded silence shows reverent attention to "the voice of God among them" since "it does not become us to speak when God is speaking."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout: Hebrews 11:30 (faith conquering), Job 15:25 (opposing God), Psalm 21:13 (God's strength), 1 Samuel 4:3 (ark misuse), Deuteronomy 1:28 (walled cities), 2 Corinthians 10:4-5 (spiritual weapons), Numbers 10:9 (trumpet warfare), Zechariah 2:13 (silence before God), and John 5:11 (Sabbath healing precedent).

The commentary transforms this historical account into rich theological instruction about divine sovereignty, patient faith, and spiritual warfare principles that transcend the immediate military context.
