## Matthew Henry's Commentary on Joshua Chapter 2: Summary

### **Main Theological Themes and Interpretations**

Henry emphasizes **divine providence** as the central theme, noting how God orchestrated events to direct the spies to Rahab's house: "that which seems to us most contingent and accidental is often over-ruled by the divine providence to serve its great ends." He presents Rahab as a remarkable example of **redemptive grace**, highlighting that "the greatness of sin is no bar to pardoning mercy if it be truly repented of."

The commentary celebrates **faith in action**, drawing from New Testament references where Rahab is "twice celebrated...as a great believer (Heb. xi. 31) and as one whose faith proved itself by good works, James ii. 25." Henry argues that true faith requires courage and risk-taking: "Those only are true believers that can find in their hearts to venture for God."

### **Key Practical Applications**

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Diligent Preparation**: "Faith in God's promise ought not to supersede but encourage our diligence in the use of proper means." Joshua's spy mission demonstrates that trusting God doesn't eliminate careful planning.

**Past Sins and Present Grace**: Using Rahab's example, Henry teaches that "even those that through grace have repented of the sins of their youth must expect to bear the reproach of them" while emphasizing God's transformative power.

**Hospitality to God's People**: Believers should show "readiness to shelter his people when there is occasion," taking God's people as their own people.

### **Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights**

Henry provides detailed analysis of Rahab's moral dilemma regarding her deception. While acknowledging her lie was problematic, he argues it was justified because "her obligations to God were higher than her obligations to any other." However, he carefully notes this was "altogether extraordinary, and therefore cannot be drawn into a precedent."

He observes Rahab's industry from the flax on her roof, suggesting she had reformed from her past: "From this instance of her honest industry one would hope that, whatever she had been formerly, she was not now a harlot."

### **Cross-References to Other Scripture**

Henry connects this narrative to broader biblical themes through multiple references: Moses's earlier spy mission (Numbers 13), Jesus's teaching about "publicans and harlots entering into the kingdom" (Matthew 21:31), the virtuous woman of Proverbs 31:13, and Isaiah 16:3-4 about sheltering outcasts. He particularly emphasizes the New Testament celebration of Rahab's faith in Hebrews 11:31 and James 2:25.

The commentary concludes by noting God's acceptance of sincere but imperfect service: "God accepts what is sincerely and honestly intended, though there be a mixture of frailty and folly in it."
