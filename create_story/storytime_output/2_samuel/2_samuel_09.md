# Story Time — 2 Samuel Chapter 9

---

The echoes of war have faded. Peace blankets the kingdom of Israel. King David sits upon his throne, a king who remembers. Years have passed since Jonathan, his dear friend and Saul’s son, fell in battle. But friendship and promises do not fade with time for David. **A covenant once made is a covenant kept.**

David's heart stirs with a question: "Is there anyone left of the house of Saul to whom I can show kindness for Jonathan’s sake?" It's more than a gesture of goodwill—it's a search for an opportunity to reflect the "kindness of God," to reach out with mercy.

A whisper travels through the palace halls—there is one, Mephibosheth, Jonathan’s son, crippled in both feet. He lives in obscurity, in the house of Machir, in a far-off place called Lo Debar. The name itself speaks of nothingness, barrenness. Yet in this forgotten corner, hope stirs.

---

Ziba, a former servant of Saul’s household, is brought before the king. David's words are direct, urgent: "Where is he?" The king’s desire to extend kindness is not passive; it is active, seeking, relentless.

Mephibosheth is summoned. He comes trembling, uncertain of his fate. Before the king, he bows low, a picture of humility, his heart no doubt weighed by years of hardship and fear of royal retribution.

And then David speaks, his voice warm and certain. "Do not fear," he says, "for I will surely show you kindness for Jonathan your father’s sake, and will restore to you all the land of Saul your grandfather; and you shall eat bread at my table continually."

**Kindness unasked** for, kindness freely given. David, in this moment of generosity, mirrors the heart of God—his covenant is one of restoration, of bringing back the forgotten, the cast aside, to a place of honor at the king's table.

---

Mephibosheth lifts his eyes, astonishment mixed with gratitude. "What is your servant, that you should look upon such a dead dog as I am?" Humility pours from his lips. In the eyes of men, he may see himself as insignificant, but in the eyes of the king, he is cherished.

David's actions here are a teaching of forgiveness in action. He does not repay Saul's aggression with more bloodshed, but instead blesses Saul's descendant. **How often do we hold onto grievances, when grace offers freedom?**

As Mephibosheth settles into his new life, sitting at the king's table as one of the king's sons, we see the truth: **the kingdom of God is as much about inclusion as it is about power.** 

---

In this chapter, we see covenant faithfulness, the honoring of promises made long ago. It asks us to remember and care for those who cannot repay us, to act in kindness reflecting God’s own nature. David's story is a reminder that true strength is found not in might, but in mercy, not in taking, but in giving.

Grace reaches into the remote corners of Lo Debar, and such is the grace of God—ever reaching, ever restoring.
