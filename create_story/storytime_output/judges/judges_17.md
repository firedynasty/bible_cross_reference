# Story Time — Judges Chapter 17

## Setting the Scene

In the land of Israel, the air is thick with uncertainty. The people have long since settled into their lands after Joshua's conquests, but now they feel adrift without a king to guide them. The absence of central leadership has left a vacuum. Into this void, chaos begins to seep.

We find ourselves staring at a modest home in the hills of Ephraim. Inside, a family drama unfolds—one that reveals the spiritual turmoil of a nation devoid of direction. It's a story of theft, of misguided devotion, and of a dangerous slide into idolatry.

## The Family Drama Begins

Meet Micah. A man of Ephraim, who stands at the crossing of desperation and opportunism. He has stolen 1,100 pieces of silver from his own mother. But fear grips him when she curses the thief. Driven by a mix of fear and guilt, Micah confesses.

"Oh, my son!" she exclaims, suddenly embracing him with relief. "Blessed be you of the Lord!"

The curse turns to a blessing, and in a twist, she dedicates some of the silver to make a graven image and a molten image—a clear violation of the commandments given to their forefathers.

But what does Micah do? He takes the remaining silver and makes a house of gods. **Is he seeking redemption, or merely trying to sanctify his ill-gotten gains?** He crafts an ephod and teraphim, constructing a private shrine, complete with his son as priest.

## A Society Adrift

There is a repeated refrain: "In those days there was no king in Israel." It's a refrain that echoes the moral vacuum of the times. Each man did what he saw fit. 

Micah's actions reveal a heart misguided, seeking divine favor through tangible idols rather than the invisible God of their ancestors. Here, Henry's insight rings true: "The beginning of idolatry, as of other sins, is like the letting forth of water." Once loosed, this deluge of spiritual decay finds no end.

Notably, Micah's mother uses only a fraction of the silver for the idols, while the rest remains hidden—a nod to covetousness, that undercurrent subtly twisting motives and actions. 

## A Wayward Priest

Enter a wandering Levite from Bethlehem-Judah, looking for a place to stay. Micah jumps at the chance, offering him the position of priest in his household gods' shrine, alongside a modest stipend and clothing.

**Isn't it ironic?** A Levite, meant to serve the Lord, finds himself serving a house of idols. Micah, in his satisfaction, believes the Lord will surely do him good now. How tragically mistaken he is.

## The Heart of the Story

Verse 6 comes into sharp focus: "In those days there was no king in Israel, but every man did that which was right in his own eyes." Here, the heart of the moral and spiritual decay beats strongest. It's a lament, a cry for order amidst the chaos. Without godly governance, the people wander into shadows.

Henry warns us: when good ministers are abandoned, when worship devolves into personal preference, the seeds of downfall are sown. As Micah mimics true worship, he strays further from the heart of God’s commands.

## Closing Reflection

Judges 17 paints a vivid picture of what happens when people lose sight of true worship. It's a story of spiritual drift, where human desires create gods in their own image. This chapter is a cautionary tale, urging us to cling to true worship and righteous leadership. In the absence of godly guidance, chaos invariably reigns.

**But what of our hearts today?** Are we crafting idols, too, when we should be seeking the face of God?
