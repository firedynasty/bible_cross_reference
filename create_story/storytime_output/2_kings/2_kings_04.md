# Story Time — 2 Kings Chapter 4

---

## Setting the Scene

The air is thick with anticipation as Elisha, the prophet of God, moves through the land. Israel is a place of turmoil and need, yet where Elisha walks, miracles seem to follow—like whispering echoes of divine assurance. At the heart of this story are the humble homes of the faithful, where ordinary lives intersect with extraordinary acts of God.

## The Widow's Oil

We begin with a cry for help. A widow stands before Elisha, her voice trembling as she recounts her plight. Her husband, a man of God, is gone. And now, the creditors threaten to take her sons as slaves.

Elisha listens, eyes solemn but kind. He asks a simple question, "What do you have in your house?"

"Nothing," she replies, "except a small jar of olive oil."

**Ah, here lies the heart of faith—beginning with what little we have.** Elisha instructs her to gather empty jars from her neighbors, not a few, but many. 

In the small, dim room of her home, the widow begins to pour. And pour. And pour. The oil flows unceasingly, filling every borrowed vessel. Only when the last jar brims does the oil stop. 

**God's abundance, meeting human need, right at the edge of faith.**

Elisha tells her to sell the oil, pay her debts, and live on the rest. God’s provision is practical, transforming her desperation into sustainable hope. 

## The Shunammite Woman

The scene shifts to Shunem. Here, a well-to-do woman recognizes Elisha as a holy man and insists on offering him hospitality. She prepares a room for him, a sanctuary of rest whenever he passes by.

Grateful, Elisha seeks to bless her in return. He learns she is childless, and promises, "About this time next year, you will hold a son in your arms."

Could it be? A promise not unlike those given to Abraham and Sarah. A miraculous hope takes root in her heart.

And so it happens. The joy of a child fills their home.

## Tragedy and Resurrection

But joy is met with tragedy. The boy, now grown, suddenly collapses in the field. Carried back to his mother, he dies upon her lap. Her grief is profound, yet she clings to hope, placing him on the prophet's bed and riding with haste to find Elisha.

Elisha returns with her. In the quiet of that room, he prays, stretches himself over the boy, and breathes into him the life-giving whisper of God's power. 

The child sneezes seven times—life restored, as if waking from a gentle sleep. Here we see the **tender mercy of God**, breathing life where there was none.

## A Pot of Stew and a Hundred Men

Lastly, we find a familiar scene of resource and need. In the midst of famine, Elisha instructs to boil stew for the prophets. But a wild vine brings poison to the pot. 

Elisha, unfazed, throws in some flour, rendering the stew safe—a small miracle of daily provision.

He then feeds a hundred men with twenty loaves, and true to God's abundance, they eat and have plenty left over.

## Reflection

This chapter is rich with reminders of God’s faithfulness and provision. It shows us that His miracles are not just grand displays but deeply woven into the fabric of daily life, meeting needs, and restoring hope. Elisha's acts are a testament to how divine power works through human action, encouraging us to offer what we have, however small, in faith and trust. In God's economy, **nothing is wasted, and every vessel filled**. 

In this tapestry of grace and mercy, we learn that God is ever close, touching the ordinary with the extraordinary.
