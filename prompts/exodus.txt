
  Moses's Motivation:

  According to the source, Moses's killing of the Egyptian taskmaster stemmed from his deliberate choice "at age 40 to identify with his suffering brethren rather than enjoy Pharaoh's court." This represented what Henry calls a "holy contempt for worldly honors" — Moses rejected his privileged position in Pharaoh's household to stand with the oppressed Israelites.

  Divine Authorization:

  The commentary interprets this act as being done "by special warrant from Heaven" — suggesting Moses was acting under divine sanction rather than mere personal impulse or vigilante justice.

  The Consequences:

  However, this act had unintended consequences. When a "quarrelsome Hebrew" later challenged Moses ("Who made thee a prince?"), this rejection "delayed Israel's deliverance forty years." Henry draws a moral lesson: "Men know not what enemies they are to their own interest when they resist and despise faithful reproofs."

  The Broader Purpose:

  Moses's subsequent flight to Midian "served divine purposes" — providing him shelter from danger and preparation through the shepherd's life for deeper "communion with God," ultimately preparing him for his future role as deliverer.

  So the text presents Moses's action as both an expression of solidarity with his enslaved people and a divinely permitted act, though one whose timing was premature, necessitating his wilderness preparation before the actual exodus could begin.
