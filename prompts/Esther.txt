## Argument Against Mordecai's Self-Interest

Michael Eisenberg presents a counterargument to the notion that Mordecai was acting out of self-interest in the Book of Esther. While some suggest that Mordecai orchestrated events to gain political power through Esther's position, Eisenberg points out several factors that contradict this interpretation:

- Mordecai's initial refusal to bow to Haman was an act that put both himself and his people at risk, rather than a calculated political move
- He remained outside the palace gates even after Esther became queen, showing no immediate personal benefit from her position
- His actions consistently prioritized the survival of the Jewish people over personal gain
- When he did receive a position of authority, it was as a result of saving the king's life and Haman's downfall, not through direct manipulation

This perspective suggests that Mordecai's actions were driven by religious conviction and concern for his people's survival rather than personal ambition.

This observation raises an important point about assimilation and Jewish identity in diaspora. Even Esther, living in the Persian palace, required Mordecai's strong persuasion to act on behalf of her people, suggesting that physical and cultural distance from Jewish community can lead to a weakening of religious and cultural ties.

Mordecai's famous appeal to Esther - "who knows whether you have come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" - highlights this tension between assimilation and maintaining Jewish identity in exile. Without a centralized homeland or state structure, each generation faced the challenge of preserving their distinct identity while living dispersed among other nations.
