BOOK: Proverbs - Matthew Henry Commentary (Summarized)
================================================================================

Introduction
----------------------------------------
Henry introduces Solomon as a new author in Scripture—a king and king's son who wrote Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and Canticles (Song of Solomon). Some believe he wrote Canticles young, Proverbs in middle age, and Ecclesiastes when old. Solomon was endued with extraordinary wisdom in answer to his prayer for an understanding heart; he used this wisdom not only to govern but to instruct others.

Henry candidly notes Solomon's faults—his later apostasy (1 Kings 11)—warning that even the most useful teachers may fall. Yet we must not think worse of good instructions because those who give them do not perfectly live up to them.

Proverbs represents a new way of writing: short, self-contained sentences not connected to one another. Teaching by proverbs is: (1) ancient—the seven wise men of Greece each had famous sayings; (2) plain and easy—requiring little strain on memory or understanding; (3) profitable—the word "Mashal" (proverb) comes from a root meaning "to rule," because wise sayings have commanding influence over men. "The world is governed by proverbs."

Henry warns that the devil, world, and flesh also have their proverbs that harden men in sin. God's proverbs guard against these corrupt influences. Solomon's proverbs are not mere collections of old sayings but dictates of the Spirit of God—the very first (1:7, "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom") echoes Job 28:28. "Behold, a greater than Solomon is here."

The structure: chapters 1-9 form a preface exhorting the study of wisdom; chapters 10-24 are the first volume of proverbs; chapters 25-29 the second volume; chapter 30 is Agur's prophecy; chapter 31 is Lemuel's. Henry concludes: "The best comment on these rules is to be ruled by them."

Chapter 1: The Fear of the Lord and Wisdom's Call
----------------------------------------
The book opens stating its purpose: to give wisdom, instruction, understanding, justice, judgment, and equity. It offers subtlety to the simple, knowledge to the young, and increased learning to the wise. Henry notes: "Even wise men must hear, and not think themselves too wise to learn."

The foundational principle (v.7): "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge." This is both the most evident truth and the most necessary prerequisite—we cannot profit from instruction without holy reverence for God. "Fools despise wisdom and instruction" because they have no dread of God's wrath nor desire for His favor.

The first practical instruction: honor parents (vv.8-9). Children must hear their father's instruction and not forsake their mother's law. Such obedience is "an ornament of grace unto thy head"—divine truths are like coronets and gold chains, badges of highest honor.

The first warning: avoid bad company (vv.10-19). "My son, if sinners entice thee, consent thou not." Henry specifies highway robbers who promise "precious substance" and shared plunder. But their way leads to their own destruction: "they lay wait for their own blood." Even birds can see a net spread, but sinners rush into ruin like horses into battle. Though Solomon specifies robbery, the warning extends to all sins—drunkenness, immorality—that sinners entice others to join.

Wisdom's public proclamation (vv.20-33): Wisdom (representing conscience, civil government, divine revelation, and Christ Himself) cries in the streets and gates. She asks: "How long, ye simple ones, will ye love simplicity?" Three groups are addressed: (1) the simple who love simplicity, (2) scorners who delight in scorning, (3) fools who hate knowledge.

The invitation: "Turn you at my reproof: behold, I will pour out my spirit unto you." Grace is promised to those who turn.

The doom of the obstinate: Because they refused when called, set at nought counsel, and despised reproof—when calamity comes, "I also will laugh at your calamity." They will call, but God will not answer. "They shall eat of the fruit of their own way." Their turning away and prosperity shall destroy them.

The promise to the obedient: "Whoso hearkeneth unto me shall dwell safely, and shall be quiet from fear of evil."

Chapter 2: Seeking Wisdom and Its Preserving Power
----------------------------------------
Solomon shows how to obtain wisdom and what advantages it brings. The means: (1) receive God's words and hide His commandments; (2) incline the ear to wisdom and apply the heart to understanding; (3) cry after knowledge in prayer—"faint desires will not prevail"; (4) seek wisdom as silver, search for it as hidden treasure. "Thus diligent must we be in the use of the means of knowledge."

The result (v.5): "Then shalt thou understand the fear of the LORD, and find the knowledge of God." God is the source: "The LORD giveth wisdom: out of his mouth cometh knowledge." He lays up sound wisdom for the righteous, is a buckler to the upright, keeps the paths of judgment, and preserves the way of His saints.

Wisdom's preserving power: When wisdom enters the heart and knowledge becomes pleasant to the soul, "discretion shall preserve thee, understanding shall keep thee." It delivers from: (1) the evil man who speaks froward things, who left uprightness to walk in darkness, who rejoices in doing evil; (2) the strange woman who flatters with her words, forsakes the guide of her youth, and forgets the covenant of her God.

The warning about the adulteress is severe: "Her house inclineth unto death, and her paths unto the dead. None that go unto her return again, neither take they hold of the paths of life." Henry notes this also warns against idolatry (spiritual whoredom) and subjecting the spirit to carnal appetites.

Wisdom guides us to "walk in the way of good men, and keep the paths of the righteous." The promise: "The upright shall dwell in the land, and the perfect shall remain in it. But the wicked shall be cut off from the earth."

Chapter 3: Trust in the Lord with All Your Heart
----------------------------------------
Henry calls this "one of the most excellent chapters in all this book." Its themes: (1) Be constant in duty—keeping God's law brings length of days and peace; (2) Let not mercy and truth forsake thee—bind them about thy neck, write them on thy heart; (3) Live a life of dependence on God.

The famous verses 5-6: "Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths." Henry: "Those who know themselves cannot but find their own understanding to be a broken reed."

Further instructions: Fear the Lord and depart from evil—"it shall be health to thy navel, and marrow to thy bones." Honor the Lord with thy substance and firstfruits—"so shall thy barns be filled with plenty." Despise not the chastening of the Lord—"whom the LORD loveth he correcteth."

The value of wisdom (vv.13-20): "Happy is the man that findeth wisdom." She is more precious than rubies; length of days is in her right hand, riches and honor in her left. "Her ways are ways of pleasantness, and all her paths are peace. She is a tree of life."

Practical duties (vv.27-35): Withhold not good from those to whom it is due. Devise not evil against thy neighbor. Strive not without cause. Envy not the oppressor. "The curse of the LORD is in the house of the wicked: but he blesseth the habitation of the just."

Chapter 4: Wisdom Is the Principal Thing
----------------------------------------
Solomon passes on what David taught him: "I was my father's son, tender and only beloved in the sight of my mother. He taught me also." Though Solomon was a prince and heir to the throne, his parents "kept him to his book." Henry notes that Rehoboam, Solomon's own son, did not profit from this instruction—"thousands have got more good by Solomon's proverbs than his own son did."

The central exhortation (v.7): "Wisdom is the principal thing; therefore get wisdom: and with all thy getting get understanding." Henry: "Wisdom is the one thing needful." The promises: "Exalt her, and she shall promote thee. She shall give to thine head an ornament of grace: a crown of glory."

The warning against evil companions (vv.14-19): "Enter not into the path of the wicked. Avoid it, pass not by it, turn from it." The wicked eat the bread of wickedness and drink the wine of violence. "The way of the wicked is as darkness: they know not at what they stumble."

The charge to guard the heart (vv.20-27): "Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life." Put away a froward mouth and perverse lips. "Let thine eyes look right on." Ponder the path of thy feet. "Turn not to the right hand nor to the left: remove thy foot from evil."

Chapter 5: Warning Against Adultery
----------------------------------------
Solomon continues warning against the "strange woman." Her lips "drop as an honeycomb" and her mouth is "smoother than oil"—but "her end is bitter as wormwood, sharp as a two-edged sword. Her feet go down to death; her steps take hold on hell."

The counsel: "Remove thy way far from her, and come not nigh the door of her house." Consequences of this sin: giving one's honor to others, years to the cruel, wealth to strangers. At the last the sinner mourns: "How have I hated instruction, and my heart despised reproof!"

The remedy—conjugal love (vv.15-20): "Drink waters out of thine own cistern." Rejoice with the wife of thy youth. "Let her breasts satisfy thee at all times; and be thou ravished always with her love." Why be ravished with a strange woman?

God's omniscience (v.21): "The ways of man are before the eyes of the LORD, and he pondereth all his goings." The end of the wicked: "He shall die without instruction; and in the greatness of his folly he shall go astray."

Chapter 6: Suretyship, Slothfulness, and Seven Abominations
----------------------------------------
Caution against rash suretyship (vv.1-5): If you have become surety for a friend or stranger, "thou art snared with the words of thy mouth." Humble yourself, make haste to be delivered—"give not sleep to thine eyes" until free. Yet Henry clarifies suretyship is not always unlawful; Paul became bound for Onesimus. But every man's wisdom is to keep out of debt.

Rebuke to slothfulness (vv.6-11): "Go to the ant, thou sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise." The ant has no guide, overseer, or ruler, yet provides her meat in summer. "How long wilt thou sleep, O sluggard?" A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands—"so shall thy poverty come as one that travelleth."

The mischievous man (vv.12-15): He walks with a froward mouth, winks with his eyes, speaks with his feet, teaches with his fingers. "Frowardness is in his heart, he deviseth mischief continually." Therefore his calamity shall come suddenly; he shall be broken without remedy.

Seven things the Lord hates (vv.16-19): "A proud look, a lying tongue, hands that shed innocent blood, a heart that deviseth wicked imaginations, feet that be swift in running to mischief, a false witness that speaketh lies, and he that soweth discord among brethren."

Warning against adultery renewed (vv.20-35): Keep thy father's commandment as a lamp, the law as light, reproofs as the way of life—"to keep thee from the evil woman." Can a man take fire in his bosom and not be burned? "Whoso committeth adultery with a woman lacketh understanding." Jealousy is the rage of a man; he will not spare in the day of vengeance.

Chapter 7: The Snare of the Seductress
----------------------------------------
The antidote: Keep God's commandments "as the apple of thine eye." Bind them upon thy fingers, write them on thy heart. "Say unto wisdom, Thou art my sister"—that she may keep thee from the strange woman.

Solomon's observation (vv.6-23): Looking through his window, he saw among the simple ones a young man "void of understanding" passing near her corner. She met him in the attire of a harlot, "subtil of heart"—loud, stubborn, feet not abiding in her house. She caught him, kissed him, said: "I have peace offerings with me; this day have I payed my vows." She decked her bed with coverings of tapestry, perfumed it with myrrh, aloes, cinnamon. "Come, let us take our fill of love... my husband is not at home." She forced him with her much fair speech.

"He goeth after her straightway, as an ox goeth to the slaughter, or as a fool to the correction of the stocks." He knew not it was for his life. Henry: "Many strong men have been slain by her. Her house is the way to hell."

Chapter 8: Wisdom's Call and Eternal Nature
----------------------------------------
Wisdom cries publicly (vv.1-11): "Doth not wisdom cry?" She stands on high places, at the gates, at the city entry. "Unto you, O men, I call." Her words are excellent things, right things, truth. "Receive my instruction, and not silver; and knowledge rather than choice gold. For wisdom is better than rubies."

Wisdom's nature and benefits (vv.12-21): "I wisdom dwell with prudence." Counsel, sound wisdom, understanding, and strength are hers. "By me kings reign, and princes decree justice." She loves those that love her; those who seek early shall find her. Riches and honor are with her—durable riches and righteousness. "I lead in the way of righteousness... that I may cause those that love me to inherit substance."

Wisdom's eternal pre-existence (vv.22-31): "The LORD possessed me in the beginning of his way, before his works of old." Henry notes many ancients understood this as referring to Christ, the eternal Word. "I was set up from everlasting, from the beginning." Before the mountains, before the hills, before the earth. "When he prepared the heavens, I was there." Wisdom was "daily his delight, rejoicing always before him; rejoicing in the habitable part of his earth; and my delights were with the sons of men."

The conclusion (vv.32-36): "Blessed is the man that heareth me, watching daily at my gates." He that finds wisdom finds life and obtains favor of the Lord. "But he that sinneth against me wrongeth his own soul: all they that hate me love death."

Chapter 9: Wisdom's Feast vs. Folly's Snare
----------------------------------------
Henry: "Christ and sin are rivals for the soul of man." Both are presented making invitations—the contrast is stark.

Wisdom's invitation (vv.1-12): "Wisdom hath builded her house, she hath hewn out her seven pillars." She has prepared a feast—killed her beasts, mingled her wine, furnished her table. "Whoso is simple, let him turn in hither... Come, eat of my bread, and drink of the wine which I have mingled. Forsake the foolish, and live."

On reproving: "Reprove not a scorner, lest he hate thee: rebuke a wise man, and he will love thee. Give instruction to a wise man, and he will be yet wiser."

The great maxim repeated (v.10): "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom: and the knowledge of the holy is understanding."

The personal choice (v.12): "If thou be wise, thou shalt be wise for thyself: but if thou scornest, thou alone shalt bear it."

Folly's invitation (vv.13-18): The foolish woman is "simple, and knoweth nothing." She sits at her door, calling to passengers: "Whoso is simple, let him turn in hither." Her enticement: "Stolen waters are sweet, and bread eaten in secret is pleasant." But "he knoweth not that the dead are there; and that her guests are in the depths of hell."

Chapters 10-16: The Proverbs Proper Begin
----------------------------------------
Henry notes: "Hitherto we have been in the porch or preface to the proverbs, here they begin." These are short, weighty sentences—distichs contrasting good and evil, blessing and curse. Key themes from these chapters:

**Chapter 10** - On family and speech: "A wise son maketh a glad father: but a foolish son is the heaviness of his mother." "Treasures of wickedness profit nothing: but righteousness delivereth from death." "The tongue of the just is as choice silver." "In the multitude of words there wanteth not sin: but he that refraineth his lips is wise."

**Chapter 11** - On honesty and generosity: "A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight." "When pride cometh, then cometh shame: but with the lowly is wisdom." "The integrity of the upright shall guide them." "There is that scattereth, and yet increaseth; and there is that withholdeth more than is meet, but it tendeth to poverty." "He that trusteth in his riches shall fall."

**Chapter 12** - On instruction and diligence: "Whoso loveth instruction loveth knowledge: but he that hateth reproof is brutish." "A virtuous woman is a crown to her husband: but she that maketh ashamed is as rottenness in his bones." "The way of a fool is right in his own eyes: but he that hearkeneth unto counsel is wise." "The hand of the diligent shall bear rule: but the slothful shall be under tribute."

**Chapter 13** - On speech and discipline: "A wise son heareth his father's instruction: but a scorner heareth not rebuke." "He that keepeth his mouth keepeth his life: but he that openeth wide his lips shall have destruction." "Hope deferred maketh the heart sick: but when the desire cometh, it is a tree of life." "He that spareth his rod hateth his son: but he that loveth him chasteneth him betimes."

**Chapter 14** - On wisdom and foolishness: "Every wise woman buildeth her house: but the foolish plucketh it down with her hands." "He that walketh in his uprightness feareth the LORD." "There is a way which seemeth right unto a man, but the end thereof are the ways of death." "In the fear of the LORD is strong confidence." "He that is slow to wrath is of great understanding."

**Chapter 15** - On the tongue and God's omniscience: "A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger." "The tongue of the wise useth knowledge aright." "The eyes of the LORD are in every place, beholding the evil and the good." "A merry heart maketh a cheerful countenance." "Better is a little with the fear of the LORD than great treasure and trouble therewith." "Better is a dinner of herbs where love is, than a stalled ox and hatred therewith."

**Chapter 16** - On God's sovereignty: "The preparations of the heart in man, and the answer of the tongue, is from the LORD"—man purposes, God disposes. "All the ways of a man are clean in his own eyes; but the LORD weigheth the spirits." "Commit thy works unto the LORD, and thy thoughts shall be established." "Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall." "The hoary head is a crown of glory, if it be found in the way of righteousness." "He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty."

Chapters 17-24: Continued Wisdom Sayings
----------------------------------------

**Chapter 17** - On peace and friendship: "Better is a dry morsel, and quietness therewith, than an house full of sacrifices with strife." Henry: "Love will sweeten a dry morsel, but strife will sour a house full of sacrifices." "A friend loveth at all times, and a brother is born for adversity." "A merry heart doeth good like a medicine: but a broken spirit drieth the bones." "Even a fool, when he holdeth his peace, is counted wise."

**Chapter 18** - On the tongue and the name of the Lord: "A fool hath no delight in understanding, but that his heart may discover itself." "The words of a man's mouth are as deep waters." "Death and life are in the power of the tongue." "The name of the LORD is a strong tower: the righteous runneth into it, and is safe." "Whoso findeth a wife findeth a good thing, and obtaineth favour of the LORD."

**Chapter 19** - On poverty and integrity: "Better is the poor that walketh in his integrity, than he that is perverse in his lips, and is a fool." "The foolishness of man perverteth his way: and his heart fretteth against the LORD." "He that hath pity upon the poor lendeth unto the LORD." "Chasten thy son while there is hope, and let not thy soul spare for his crying." "The fear of the LORD tendeth to life."

**Chapter 20** - On wine and diligence: "Wine is a mocker, strong drink is raging: and whosoever is deceived thereby is not wise." Henry: "Drunkenness makes a fool of a man." "The sluggard will not plow by reason of the cold; therefore shall he beg in harvest, and have nothing." "Counsel in the heart of man is like deep water; but a man of understanding will draw it out." "Who can say, I have made my heart clean, I am pure from my sin?"

**Chapter 21** - On God's sovereignty over kings: "The king's heart is in the hand of the LORD, as the rivers of water: he turneth it whithersoever he will." Henry: "Kings are what God makes them." "Every way of a man is right in his own eyes: but the LORD pondereth the hearts." "To do justice and judgment is more acceptable to the LORD than sacrifice." "It is better to dwell in a corner of the housetop, than with a brawling woman in a wide house."

**Chapter 22** - On a good name and training children: "A good name is rather to be chosen than great riches, and loving favour rather than silver and gold." "The rich and poor meet together: the LORD is the maker of them all." "Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it." "The borrower is servant to the lender." "Rob not the poor, because he is poor: neither oppress the afflicted in the gate."

**Chapter 23** - On temperance and parental joy: "When thou sittest to eat with a ruler, consider diligently what is before thee." "Labour not to be rich: cease from thine own wisdom." "Buy the truth, and sell it not; also wisdom, and instruction, and understanding." "My son, give me thine heart, and let thine eyes observe my ways." On wine (vv.29-35): "Who hath woe? who hath sorrow?... They that tarry long at the wine... At the last it biteth like a serpent, and stingeth like an adder."

**Chapter 24** - On wisdom and diligence: "Be not thou envious against evil men... for their heart studieth destruction." "Through wisdom is an house builded; and by understanding it is established." "If thou faint in the day of adversity, thy strength is small." "Rejoice not when thine enemy falleth." "By much slothfulness the building decayeth." "Yet a little sleep, a little slumber... so shall thy poverty come as one that travelleth."

Chapters 25-29: Hezekiah's Collection
----------------------------------------
Henry notes: "These are also proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out." About 250 years after Solomon, Hezekiah's servants—perhaps prophets like Isaiah, Hosea, or Micah—gathered and preserved these additional proverbs. Key sayings:

**Chapter 25** - On kings and conduct: "It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter." "Put not forth thyself in the presence of the king." "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in pictures of silver." "If thine enemy be hungry, give him bread to eat; and if he be thirsty, give him water to drink: for thou shalt heap coals of fire upon his head" (quoted by Paul in Romans 12:20).

**Chapter 26** - On fools and sluggards: "Answer not a fool according to his folly, lest thou also be like unto him. Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own conceit." Henry explains both are true in different circumstances. "As a dog returneth to his vomit, so a fool returneth to his folly." "Seest thou a man wise in his own conceit? there is more hope of a fool than of him." "Where no wood is, there the fire goeth out: so where there is no talebearer, the strife ceaseth."

**Chapter 27** - On friendship and self-knowledge: "Boast not thyself of to morrow; for thou knowest not what a day may bring forth." "Let another man praise thee, and not thine own mouth." "Faithful are the wounds of a friend; but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful." "Iron sharpeneth iron; so a man sharpeneth the countenance of his friend." "Hell and destruction are never full; so the eyes of man are never satisfied."

**Chapter 28** - On the righteous and wicked: "The wicked flee when no man pursueth: but the righteous are bold as a lion." "He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy." "He that hasteth to be rich hath an evil eye." "He that trusteth in his own heart is a fool: but whoso walketh wisely, he shall be delivered."

**Chapter 29** - On rulers and the fear of man: "He, that being often reproved hardeneth his neck, shall suddenly be destroyed, and that without remedy." "When the righteous are in authority, the people rejoice: but when the wicked beareth rule, the people mourn." "The fear of man bringeth a snare: but whoso putteth his trust in the LORD shall be safe." "Where there is no vision, the people perish: but he that keepeth the law, happy is he."

Chapter 30: The Words of Agur
----------------------------------------
This chapter is "an appendix to Solomon's proverbs" written by "Agur Ben Jakeh"—called a prophecy, indicating divine inspiration. Henry notes the structure features groups of four:

Agur's confession (vv.1-6): He confesses ignorance and asks: "Who hath ascended up into heaven, or descended?" and "What is his name, and what is his son's name?" Henry sees here "an intimation of the mystery of the Incarnation." "Every word of God is pure... Add thou not unto his words, lest he reprove thee, and thou be found a liar."

Agur's prayer (vv.7-9): "Two things have I required of thee... Remove far from me vanity and lies: give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with food convenient for me: lest I be full, and deny thee, and say, Who is the LORD? or lest I be poor, and steal, and take the name of my God in vain." Henry: "A wise and good man will see occasion to limit his own desires."

Groups of four: Four things insatiable (the grave, barren womb, earth not filled with water, fire). Four things unsearchable (the way of an eagle, serpent, ship, man with a maid). Four things intolerable (a servant reigning, a fool filled with meat, an odious woman married, a handmaid heir to her mistress). Four things little yet wise (ants, conies, locusts, spiders). Four things stately (lion, greyhound, he-goat, a king against whom there is no rising up).

Chapter 31: King Lemuel and the Virtuous Woman
----------------------------------------
Henry notes most interpreters believe Lemuel is Solomon, taught by his mother (perhaps Bathsheba). The name means "one devoted to God."

A mother's counsel to a prince (vv.1-9): "What, my son? and what, the son of my womb? and what, the son of my vows?" She warns against women who destroy kings and wine that perverts judgment. Instead: "Open thy mouth for the dumb... Open thy mouth, judge righteously, and plead the cause of the poor and needy."

The virtuous woman (vv.10-31): This famous acrostic poem (each verse beginning with successive Hebrew letters) describes the ideal wife. "Who can find a virtuous woman? for her price is far above rubies." Her husband safely trusts in her. "She seeketh wool, and flax, and worketh willingly with her hands." She rises while still night, considers a field and buys it, girds her loins with strength. "She stretcheth out her hand to the poor." Her husband is known in the gates. "Strength and honour are her clothing."

Her speech: "She openeth her mouth with wisdom; and in her tongue is the law of kindness." Her family praises her: "Her children arise up, and call her blessed; her husband also, and he praiseth her."

The conclusion: "Many daughters have done virtuously, but thou excellest them all. Favour is deceitful, and beauty is vain: but a woman that feareth the LORD, she shall be praised. Give her of the fruit of her hands; and let her own works praise her in the gates."
